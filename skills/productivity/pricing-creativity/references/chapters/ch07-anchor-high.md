## Core idea

Always include an option priced above where the client is expected to buy, and
present it first. The anchor's job is not to be bought but to make the middle
option, the one the firm wants to sell, look like good value.

## Frameworks

- **Rule #3, anchor high**: anchor every multi-option proposal with the most
  expensive option, priced above the expected purchase.
  - When: every multi-option proposal, and every early conversation about price.
  - How:
    1. Ask what the firm would do, and charge, if this client had unlimited
       resources. Deliberately set aside beliefs about what the client will
       spend. The answer is the anchor.
    2. Price the low option within the client's stated budget or expectation.
    3. Price the middle option, the high-margin one the firm wants to sell,
       above the low option, inside the budget or not.
    4. Lay the options out from low to high on the page, but start with the
       anchor when walking the client through them.
    5. In early conversations, say the high end of the range first (see
       anchoring against guaranteed value in ch09).
  - Why: anchoring and adjusting (Kahneman and Tversky). The fast, intuitive
    mind grabs the first number; the slow, deliberate mind adjusts away from it
    but stops at the near edge of its uncertainty, so the adjustment falls
    short. It works even when the target knows it is happening.
  - Failure mode: presenting the anchor last. That uses only extremeness
    aversion. True anchoring needs priming, so the anchor must be the first
    price the client hears.
- **Reading the outcome**:
  - The anchor is chosen 20% of the time or more: the anchor and the middle
    option are both priced too low.
  - The client always picks the low option: the gap in price or value between
    the low and middle options is too big.
  - The middle option is chosen: that's the goal. The low option: fine, with
    slight regret. The anchor: celebrate.
- **Prices before solutions**: the value chain runs Client, Value, Price, Cost,
  Solutions. After the value conversation, set three or four prices first, work
  back to the most worth spending on each, and only then design the solutions.
  If that feels backward, it is a habit left over from cost-based pricing.

## Concepts

- **Anchor**: the highest-priced option; it shifts the client's mental reference
  point upward rather than being bought.
- **Anchoring and adjusting**: people start from the first number given and
  adjust too little away from it.
- **Priming**: planting the first piece of information. Anchoring is a form of
  priming, which is why order matters.
- **Extremeness aversion**: the pull away from the highest and lowest options
  toward the middle; weaker than true anchoring.
- **Social proof**: marking an option as most popular steers buyers toward it.
- **Negotiating status**: the standing to set the anchor rather than respond to
  one. The old advice that whoever names a number first loses is exactly
  backward.
- **Blunting an anchor**: the few things that weaken an anchor are making the
  other side withdraw and restate it, visibly rejecting it on hearing it, and
  focusing on what the other side loses if there is no deal. Merely countering
  with a lower number doesn't work.
- **Expensive items sell the next one down**: a luxury brand's $500 T-shirt
  exists to sell its $275 T-shirts.
- **Bolder asks win more**: in litigation, larger demands tend to bring larger
  awards, with no sign of backlash even for very large ones.

## Anti-patterns

- **Letting the client speak first on price**: their low number becomes the
  reference point for the whole negotiation.
- **Presenting the anchor last, or low to high**: forfeits priming and leaves
  only the weaker middle-option effect.
- **Keeping every option inside the stated budget**: there is no such
  requirement, and research shows no backlash from a too-high anchor.
- **Pricing the anchor to be bought**: if it sells 20% of the time or more, the
  firm has under-anchored and underpriced the middle option.
- **Designing solutions before prices**: a return to the cost-based chain.

## Example

A client's budget is about $20k. In the first conversation, lead with a $100k
case study, which shows depth and plants the anchor. Follow with a $50k case
study, which now looks reasonable. A $30k option then looks like a bargain next
to $100k, even though it is half again the client's budget. Salesforce does the
same: its top tier, at about $300 a month, sells to under 1% of customers but
makes the $150 tier look sensible, and a most-popular badge nudges buyers there.

## Takeaways

1. Give every proposal three or four options, with the top one deliberately
   above what the client is likely to spend.
2. Set the anchor by pricing for a hypothetical client with unlimited resources;
   ignore what this client can afford.
3. Say the anchor first in proposals, case studies, and early range guidance.
   The first number heard wins.
4. The middle option is the target, the low option sits inside the budget, and
   the anchor exists to sell the middle option.
5. Track which option clients choose. Frequent anchor picks mean raise prices;
   constant low-option picks mean close the gap.
6. Set prices before designing what the firm will do for them.
