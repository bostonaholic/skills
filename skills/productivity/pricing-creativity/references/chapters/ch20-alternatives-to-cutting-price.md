## Core idea

A deal stalling over price does not force a discount. Six other moves exist:
hold conviction in the firm's value, change the payment terms, reduce the
scope, add a guarantee, ask for a bigger commitment in exchange, or decline the
work.

## Frameworks

- **Believe**: self-belief comes before client belief. A price premium is the
  economic measure of how meaningfully different a solution is seen to be, and
  the client can't see the difference until the salesperson does.
  - When: you catch yourself building the case for a lower price.
  - How: recognize talk about price as a signal of the seller's self-esteem, not
    of the market. The top performers on large sales teams rarely talk about
    price; their higher margins and close rates come from believing in
    themselves more than in the product.
  - Failure mode: sellers who believe tend to keep believing, and sellers who
    don't tend to keep cutting price.
- **Offer terms**: separate a value objection from an affordability objection,
  and answer affordability with payment structure, not price.
  - Signal: the client says they don't doubt the value, they just can't afford
    it.
  - How: if paying the full amount at once is the problem, offer a payment plan,
    and structure the terms so they earn the firm more over time (in ch15, a
    client who couldn't afford $50k ended up paying $108k).
  - Terms that never bend: a sizable deposit from every new client before work
    starts (at a large company a purchase order may do; otherwise wait for the
    check). If the firm carries the client's costs up front, as with a lease or
    license, have a lawyer draft a solid contract and build in a healthy profit
    over time.
  - Note: the ability to accept onerous terms can itself be a competitive
    advantage (one firm effectively lent money to large corporate clients), but
    don't set out to compete that way.
- **Take out some value**: if a good client won't meet the price and the firm
  still wants the deal, take less money only if the client gives something up.
  - Why: a skilled negotiator reads a price cut without a value cut as proof
    that more discounts are available, and is trained to keep asking until
    hearing no.
  - Rule: keep this for good clients who want the higher-priced options, and
    think hard before doing it on the low option. With price buyers, point to
    the affordable option and hold firm.
- **Use guarantees**: when the objection is risk (the price seems high and the
  client worries it won't work), guarantee the first phase to hold the price.
  - How: promise that if, after the firm presents its findings and
    recommendations, the client feels it was a mistake, they can end the
    engagement and get their money back.
  - Rules: the client pays for the whole first phase up front
    (pay-upfront-with-a-guarantee, not pay what you like), so loss aversion
    works for the firm. Extend the guarantee as far as creative concepts and
    think twice beyond that. Keep conditions minimal; Enns's only one is that
    the principal stays personally committed rather than handing the work to
    subordinates.
  - Why: far less risky than the common alternative, pitching free work.
- **Trade for a larger commitment** (with care): a lower price for a written
  volume commitment is logically the same trade as removing value, but most
  creative firms have few real economies of scale, so volume discounts demanded
  by procurement are usually absurd.
  - Signal: the procurement squeeze for a volume discount typically arrives a
    year or more into a relationship, with no new volume on offer, only an
    implied threat to existing work. Use another alternative.
- **Walk away**: the firm is never worse off for walking away from a tough
  negotiation or a price buyer.
  - Why: it preserves integrity and the expert practitioner position, and keeps
    the option to re-engage. Walking away is often a pause, and retreating tends
    to draw the other side toward you. Reach out again in a few days with a new
    structure, or wait for the buyer to blink.
  - Mindset: a no is the start of what happens next, not the end. Everyone
    eventually buys; only the timing varies.

## Concepts

- **Price premium**: the economic measure of perceived meaningful difference,
  usually lower risk.
- **Affordability objection**: solved with terms, not price.
- **Non-negotiable terms**: a deposit before work starts, from every new client.
- **Pay-upfront-with-a-guarantee**: full payment for phase one plus a money-back
  promise; distinct from pay what you like.
- **Volume discount**: usually fictitious at creative firms, which have no
  economies of scale to pass on.
- **Revenue certainty**: the only legitimate thing to trade price for in a
  commitment deal, and only in writing.
- **Matching the remedy to the problem**: terms for cash flow, removed value for
  unwillingness, a guarantee for risk.

## Anti-patterns

- **Cutting price without removing value**: tells the negotiator there is more
  to extract.
- **Starting work for a new client without a deposit**: an uncommitted client
  and all the risk on the firm.
- **Free pitch work instead of a guarantee**: more risk and a weaker signal.
- **Conceding a volume discount to keep existing volume**: no new revenue
  changes hands.
- **Treating a walk-away as final**: it is usually a pause.

## Example

A client says they don't doubt the value but can't afford it. Instead of
dropping the $50k price, the firm treats affordability as the problem to solve
and asks what payment schedule would work. It structures a lease-style plan that
totals $108k over the term, takes a deposit before work begins, and has a lawyer
draft a contract covering its up-front costs. The client gets affordability, and
the firm earns more, not less.

## Takeaways

1. Diagnose the objection first: value, affordability, or risk. Each has an
   answer that isn't a price cut.
2. Never lower price without removing value; the first unearned discount invites
   the next.
3. Require a deposit from every new client before work starts.
4. Guarantee phase one, paid up front, with minimal conditions, instead of
   pitching free work.
5. Refuse volume discounts that bring no new written volume.
6. Be willing to walk away, and come back days later with a new structure if the
   fit was right.
