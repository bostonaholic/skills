# Chapter 6: Rule #2, Offer Options

## Core idea

People can't perceive absolute value, only comparisons. A single-price proposal
sends the client looking for comparisons elsewhere: competitors, past spending,
other uses of the money. Present three options instead, so the client's question
becomes which option is the best value, and the firm controls the comparison.

## Frameworks

- **Rule #2, offer options**: every proposal a client sees includes options,
  without exception.
  - When: every proposal, value-priced ones included. A value price is a
    comparison in itself, but the client still wonders what else the same money
    could buy.
  - How: (1) assemble options and prices for this client and the value the firm
    might create (Rule #1); (2) aim for three, since two beat one and three beat
    two, four works about as well, and more risks choice overload; (3) add an
    anchor priced above the others, because adding a higher option shifts
    choices toward the middle while adding a cheaper one drags them down; (4)
    mix pricing bases freely: time and materials, deliverables, value, or one of
    each.
  - Why: options turn a question the mind can't answer (is this good value?)
    into one it can (which option is the best value?). A single
    take-it-or-leave-it offer is a coin flip; three options raise the odds of a
    positive outcome by about half, and that is the smaller benefit.
  - Failure mode: a cheaper third option lowers the average sale.
- **Extremeness aversion**: when uncertain, buyers avoid the highest and lowest
  options. With options at $1,000 and $1,250, adding $1,500 sends more buyers to
  $1,250; adding $750 instead pulls both the $1,250 share and the average price
  down.
- **Decoys**: options at the edges of the range that exist to steer the buyer,
  not to be sold. An unattractive option makes its neighbor look attractive.
- **Choice architecture**: the firm already frames every choice it presents,
  whether it admits it or not, so it should learn to frame well.

## Concepts

- **Absolute vs. comparative value**: value is known only by measuring against a
  reference.
- **Comparative value**: even a good ROI has to beat the client's other uses for
  the money.
- **Opportunity cost**: the alternatives the client silently compares the firm
  to.
- **Controlling the comparison**: supply the comparisons yourself so the client
  never needs to leave, mentally or physically, to decide.
- **Choice overload**: anxiety rises with too many options. The research is
  contested, but the effect shows up on real menus.
- **Chosen things are liked more** (Rory Sutherland): people gladly pay for
  shoes they picked and resent bills they never chose.
- **Context changes perception**: in Edward Adelson's checker-shadow illusion,
  two squares of identical gray look different because of their surroundings.
  Context can make an expensive price look cheap.
- **The anchor sells the middle option**: Williams-Sonoma's pricier breadmaker
  sold few units but made the cheaper model fly off the shelves.

## Anti-patterns

- **Single-option proposals**: a 50/50 gamble that also pushes the client to
  look elsewhere for comparisons.
- **Adding a cheaper third option**: pulls the average sale down.
- **Too many options**: past four, choice anxiety costs momentum.
- **Pretending the choice stands on its own**: the firm influences it either
  way, and unconscious framing is the less honest version.
- **Assuming value-priced deals need no options**: a single value price still
  leaves the client unable to judge it alone.

## Example

Enns shows audiences a photo of a stick and asks how long it is. Answers range
from inches to many feet, and most people say it is impossible to tell without a
reference. He then shows Adelson's checker-shadow illusion; told that two
squares are the same shade, he once covered the rest of the board with strips of
paper until he could see it. The pricing lesson: a client can't know whether a
$5,000 proposal is worth $5,000 until comparing it to competitors, past
purchases, or other uses of the money. Supply the comparison: three options,
highest at the top, with any decoy priced to make the preferred option the
sensible middle.

## Takeaways

1. Never send a one-price proposal. Three options is the default; four is the
   limit.
2. Make the highest-priced option the anchor, and never add a cheaper option to
   be safe.
3. Build options per client from the firm's value drivers; they may mix time and
   materials, deliverables, and value.
4. Use decoys deliberately to steer toward the option you want sold.
5. Own the framing; the choice is being shaped either way.
