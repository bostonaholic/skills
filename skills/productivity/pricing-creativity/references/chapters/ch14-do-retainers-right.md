# Chapter 14: Do Retainers Right

## Core idea

Retainers bring predictable revenue but push a firm toward cost-based pricing
and productization, and they usually leave one side feeling shortchanged. Use
them for only two things, open access (strategic guidance) and reserved
capacity, and never build one by dividing the client's annual budget by 12.

## Frameworks

- **Three benefits of retainers**, most important first:
  1. **Predictable revenue**: smooths the firm's income, and the client gets the
     same visibility into expenses. It calms nerves in tight or uncertain times,
     even when the terms are poor.
  2. **Better resource allocation**: people, space, and technology can be
     planned against known revenue, and scaled with foresight.
  3. **Better relationships**: consistent people on the firm's side deepen the
     relationship.
- **Three ways retainers fail**, biggest first:
  1. **One side is usually unhappy.** The client believes it bought price
     certainty and is paying a premium for it, but the firm doesn't actually
     carry delivery risk: if a month runs over, the firm slows down or stops,
     and the client knows it. Look at any retainer six months old or more and
     one side will feel over- or underserved. Few double thank-you moments
     happen in month nine of twelve.
  2. **Retainers drift to cost-based pricing.** Even a value-priced engagement
     split into 12 equal payments drifts toward managing efficiency, and someone
     will expect the firm to hold back resources as soon as the work exceeds the
     fee. It breeds time-and-materials thinking.
  3. **Retainers push the firm toward productized services.** When two clients
     pay a similar monthly amount for similar work, someone proposes packaging
     it on the website. That breaks Rule #1, and the firm stops asking clients
     what hurts and what they are trying to achieve.
- **Two sound uses of retainers**:
  1. **Open access (strategic guidance)**: describe it as open access in the
     proposal, and add it to the higher-priced options. Following Alan Weiss,
     offer a meaningful discount (10% or more) for 12 months paid in advance.
     That brings cash up front and removes eleven monthly moments when the
     client wonders what the firm has done lately.
  2. **Reserved capacity**: clients with large, sporadic tactical needs pay to
     keep capacity on hold, the original logic of the agency of record. Work out
     what the reservation is worth to both sides.
  - Why these work: the client pays for access or for held capacity, things it
    really wants, rather than for time, which keeps the arrangement from sliding
    into cost accounting.
  - Failure mode: a retainer that is really a monthly bucket of hours is a
    time-and-materials contract with a nicer name.
- **Three questions before proposing a retainer**:
  - Is the firm overvaluing revenue certainty above everything else?
  - Is this right for both parties?
  - Is the firm choosing a retainer because it is easier to sell?

## Concepts

- **Retainer, original sense**: an advance payment, invented by lawyers, that
  reserves a place on a professional's roster and blocks conflicts.
- **Retainer, current sense**: a fixed monthly fee, usually for 12 months, and
  almost always cost-based in practice.
- **Price certainty**: what the client believes it is buying; it earns a premium
  only when the firm actually carries the delivery risk. A retainer is a promise
  of price certainty the firm hasn't underwritten, and that imbalance sours it.
- **Customized services firm**: few clients, a unique engagement and price for
  each, and a culture of innovation; what most creative firms are and should
  stay.
- **Productized services firm**: fixed solutions sold to many customers at fixed
  prices, with a culture of efficiency and cost-based pricing.
- **Open access**: the strategic-guidance retainer, in which the client
  initiates and the firm responds.
- **Agency of record**: an engagement that reserves capacity; less common than
  it once was.
- **Budget ÷ 12**: the wrong way to build a retainer. It forces the work into
  twelve equal parts even when it is front-loaded or bursty.
- **The monthly invoice**: each one invites the client to re-evaluate the firm;
  annual prepayment removes eleven of them.

## Anti-patterns

- **Annual budget ÷ 12**: divides the work evenly even when it should be
  front-loaded or delivered in bursts.
- **Rejecting work below a retainer minimum**: a PR firm partner turned down
  $50k of very profitable two-to-three-month work because his minimum was $10k a
  month, which Enns calls lazy thinking rather than principle.
- **Retainers for everyone**: pursuing retainers at the expense of every other
  pricing model.
- **Packaging one client's retainer for the website**: the slide into
  productization.
- **Throttling delivery to protect margin**: what every retainer client
  suspects, and the source of the unhappiness.

## Example

A PR firm partner boasted of turning down a $50k engagement. Enns asked how long
the work would take; the answer was two or three months. The firm's habit was to
divide annual budgets by 12 with a $10k monthly floor, so a $50k job lasting a
couple of months (several months' worth of retainer for a short burst of work)
looked unacceptable. Some clients really do budget annually in twelfths, and
then a monthly structure may be unavoidable, but a preferred structure should
never kill a profitable deal.

## Takeaways

1. Treat retainers as one tool among many, not the goal.
2. Sell strategic guidance as open access, added to the higher-priced options,
   with 10% or more off for 12 months paid in advance.
3. Sell reserved capacity as a retainer only when the client values having
   capacity held.
4. Never build a retainer by dividing the annual budget by 12. Price the work,
   then choose a payment schedule.
5. Watch for productization creep once the firm has more than a couple of
   retainer clients.
6. Ask the three questions before every retainer proposal; a retainer that is
   merely easier to sell is the lazy kind.
