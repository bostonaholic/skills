# Chapter 13: Make the Margin in the Middle

## Core idea

Structure the three options so the client is nudged toward the middle one, and
make the middle option the firm's highest-margin offer, since that is where most
clients land. Build it from help forms (knowledge transfer, oversight, access,
standards, reporting) that cost the firm little and are worth a lot to the
client.

## Frameworks

- **Home Depot Option**: named for the retailer's slogan about customers doing
  the work themselves with the store's help. It is the middle option in a
  three-option proposal whose low option is a diagnostic (the firm says what
  needs doing) and whose anchor is full implementation (the firm does
  everything).
  - When: the client can get routine execution done cheaply, by staff or
    vendors, but won't rely on them for strategy, big ideas, or the first
    rollout of those ideas.
  - How:
    1. Low option: a diagnostic of findings, recommendations, and sometimes
       creative concepts. It is cheap relative to the other options but
       high-margin, because it is strategic.
    2. Anchor: full implementation, priced very high. When the client clearly
       won't choose it (say, under pressure to use cheaper resources), push it
       higher still to make the middle option look better.
    3. Middle option: concepts developed with the client's team (a workshop or
       collaboration) plus one or more of the help forms below. Price it as a
       bargain against the anchor, not as a markup on cost.
  - Why: firms get frustrated when clients split the engagement, because their
    only model is full implementation, with strategy underpriced and tactical
    work overpriced. Price each part properly and splitting is fine: the client
    keeps the routine work and the firm keeps the margin.
  - Failure mode: pricing the middle option by hours instead of by contrast with
    the anchor.
- **Help forms** (the ingredients of a high-margin middle option): each can be
  stacked on a diagnostic or any low option, and each can also appear in the
  anchor.
  1. **Knowledge transfer**: formal training, shadowing, or embedding client
     staff in the engagement. Ask how many ways the firm can transfer skill,
     what to charge, and whether the client can draw on a separate training
     budget.
  2. **Oversight and quality control**: creative direction, account planning, or
     project oversight of the client's in-house execution. Don't guess; ask the
     client whether a particular form of oversight would be valuable in one of
     the options.
  3. **Open access to principals**: name consulting time open access in the
     proposal, which puts the onus on the client to initiate. Include it only in
     the higher options, to signal it isn't available in the lower ones. Enns's
     floor for open access to himself was $5k a month.
  4. **Standards**: brand, wayfinding, or UX standards guides. Unbundle them
     from the base engagement; strip them from the low option for underfunded
     clients and include them in the middle option and anchor.
  5. **Reporting**: daily check-ins, weekly meetings, monthly written reports.
     Clients value different cadences; leave light reporting in the low option
     and richer reporting in the middle option and anchor.

## Concepts

- **Middle option**: the target option. Safe from both extremes, it is where
  most clients land, so make landing there very profitable for the firm.
- **Diagnostic**: a low-priced option delivering findings and recommendations;
  strategic, so always high-margin despite its low relative price.
- **Anchor**: here, the full-implementation option; its job is to make the
  middle option look cheap. The less likely the client is to choose it, the
  higher it should go.
- **Open access**: the paid right to call or email a principal and expect a
  response; consulting reframed so the client initiates. If the client doesn't
  buy it, the firm keeps the attention; if they do, it gets the revenue. Being
  glad either way is the sign it is priced right.
- **Knowledge transfer**: paid transfer of the firm's know-how to client staff.
  Welcome clients who pay more now so they need the firm less later.
- **Double thank-you**: a deal where both sides feel they won; here, the client
  manages the work for far less than the anchor and the firm books mostly
  profit.
- **Unbundling**: pulling standards, reporting, access, and similar drivers out
  of the base engagement so they become priced value in the higher options.

## Anti-patterns

- **Underpricing strategy and overpricing tactics**: the default
  full-implementation model, which leaves the firm exposed when the client keeps
  execution in-house.
- **Treating open access as free**: neither side should assume unlimited access
  to the firm's senior people comes included.
- **One reporting cadence for everyone**: wastes a lever that steers clients
  toward the middle option.
- **Guessing at oversight needs**: ask the client while assembling the options.
- **Refusing to unbundle standards**: leaves revenue on the table and blocks a
  low option for underfunded clients.

## Example

A small marketing firm's client wanted creative concepts for experiential
marketing, developed with the client's in-house team in a three-day workshop
(two people on site, about six person-days of delivery plus a couple of days of
preparation).

- **Low option (diagnostic)**: the firm develops the concepts alone and hands
  them over.
- **Middle option (Home Depot)**: a three-day collaborative workshop in which
  the client's team learns alongside the experts, priced at $175k (about $30k
  per person per day).
- **Anchor**: full implementation, priced at about $5M, mostly contractors and
  production costs.

The client saw $175k as cheap next to $5M and managed the project himself for
less than $5M in total, and most of the $175k was profit for the firm.

## Takeaways

1. Build every three-option proposal so the middle option is the target and
   carries the highest margin.
2. Use the Home Depot structure (diagnostic, help, full implementation) for
   clients with in-house teams or cheap vendors.
3. Stack help forms into the middle option and the anchor, and withhold them
   from the low option.
4. Price open access to principals explicitly; Enns's floor was $5k a month.
5. When the anchor won't sell anyway, raise it to make the middle option look
   better.
6. Ask the client which oversight or reporting they would value before
   finalizing the options.
