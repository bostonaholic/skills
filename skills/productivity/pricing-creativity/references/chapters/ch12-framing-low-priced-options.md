## Core idea

The low option should be profitable and hassle-free even if a pure price buyer
takes it. Build it as a time block with every other value driver stripped out,
and let the more expensive options show the client exactly what the low option
gives up.

## Frameworks

- **Three buyer types** (after Reed Holden):
  - **Price buyer**: buys on price, always picks the lowest option, and treats
    the purchase as an expense. The Flip never happens, the firm stays a vendor,
    and the value conversation frustrates them.
  - **Value buyer**: does at least rough ROI math and treats the purchase as an
    investment.
  - **Poker player**: a value buyer posing as a price buyer. Engages properly on
    the future state and metrics, then turns up the price sensitivity when value
    and price come up. It is a bluff, and inexperienced pricers fold by cutting
    price.
  - When: the firm often can't tell a price buyer from a poker player during the
    sale. The something-for-everyone structure below makes the distinction
    unnecessary.
- **When to work with price buyers** (most firms should, when both hold):
  1. The firm has spare junior capacity.
  2. The low option strips out every other value driver, keeping the job
     profitable and the hassle low.
- **Selling time blocks** (the legitimate use of cost-based pricing):
  - How: the low option is a block of hours, and the engagement ends when they
    are used up. No topping up by the hour; if more work is needed, the firm
    sets the minimum block size (for example, $10k). Strip out payment terms
    (100% up front), project management, reporting, senior access, and price
    certainty. The work goes to whichever junior has capacity.
  - Requirement: spell out in the pricier options everything the cheapest one
    leaves out (terms, senior people, reporting, price certainty), so the
    trade-off is plain.
  - Why: price buyers get a profitable, bounded engagement; poker players see
    what they would forgo and move to the middle option; value buyers with
    unrealistic budgets get a reality check.
- **Something-for-everyone structure**: the low option is for the price buyer (a
  time block, value stripped); the middle option is for the poker player (the
  value drivers this client wants, per Rule #1); the top is the anchor. Built
  this way, misreading the buyer type costs nothing.

## Concepts

- **Price buyer**: wants the lowest price, treats the firm as a vendor, and sees
  spending as expense.
- **Value buyer**: wants the best value, does ROI math, and sees spending as
  investment.
- **Poker player** (Reed Holden, _Pricing With Confidence_): a value buyer who
  negotiates hard on principle by bluffing price sensitivity.
- **Price certainty**: a value driver present in fixed-price options and absent
  from time blocks, where work stops when the hours run out.
- **Hassle cost**: the hidden cost of price-buyer clients, removed by stripping
  the low option to bare time.
- **Spare capacity**: the junior time that makes a low option worthwhile at all.
- **Minimum block**: the firm's rule for the smallest quantity of time it will
  sell.
- **Every price is a trade-off**: the firm decides what each price includes and
  excludes, and the low option works as a mirror that makes the middle option's
  value visible.

## Anti-patterns

- **Folding to a poker player by cutting price**: rewards the bluff and loses
  margin on a client who would have paid for value.
- **A low option that still includes terms, senior access, reporting, or price
  certainty**: the cheap option becomes a bargain on value, and the client
  rationally picks it.
- **Selling hours by the hour**: loses control of scope; sell fixed blocks on
  the firm's terms.
- **Refusing all price buyers by reflex**: with spare junior capacity and a
  stripped option, they can be profitable.
- **One option template for every client**: clients value different things, so
  the middle option is rebuilt each time.

## Example

A client insists on a $10k budget. You doubt it buys any certainty and can't
tell whether they are bluffing. Three options:

- **Low option (about $10k, for the price buyer)**: a block of junior hours,
  paid 100% in advance, with an initial briefing and one midpoint check-in only.
  No reporting and no senior access. When the hours run out, the engagement
  ends, and further work comes only in the firm's minimum block size (for
  example, another $10k rather than $5k).
- **Middle option (for the poker player)**: a fixed price with work continuing
  until delivery (price certainty), plus the specific value drivers this client
  showed interest in: payment terms, reporting, senior involvement.
- **Anchor**: the concierge option from ch11.

A true price buyer takes the low option and stays profitable and low-touch. A
poker player sees what $10k costs them in terms and certainty and moves to the
middle option.

## Takeaways

1. Design the low option so you're content if it is chosen: less money, but
   fewer resources and less risk.
2. Sell price buyers a time block with every other value driver stripped out,
   and only when the firm has spare junior capacity.
3. Never sell hours one at a time; the firm sets the minimum block.
4. Make the higher options spell out what the low option lacks; the comparison
   does the selling.
5. Structure the low, middle, and top options for the price buyer, the poker
   player, and the anchor, so misreading the buyer costs nothing.
6. Take the low price from the client's own guidance, and don't default to a
   time block when a value buyer's budget is realistic.
