# Chapter 2: Price Like a Marketer

Paraphrased summary of chapter 2 of Blair Enns's _Pricing Creativity_.

## Core idea

A firm's culture can pursue efficiency (a producer that takes the market's
price) or innovation and value creation (a marketer that sets its own price),
but not both. Only the marketer path lets a firm set its own prices.

## Frameworks

- **Producer-marketer spectrum**: every business makes things and sells things.
  Producers optimize production and leave market creation to others; marketers
  anticipate demand and invent offerings, often outsourcing production.
  - When: diagnosing why prices feel stuck in a narrow, low band.
  - How: (1) name the pricing pressures: client budgets, history, and
    negotiating skill; competitor prices; and the firm's own ideas of fairness.
    (2) Ask whether the firm answers them with efficiency (scale, cost cutting,
    utilization) or innovation (new offerings, more risk). (3) Place the firm on
    the spectrum and choose a direction deliberately, knowing that raising one
    lowers the other.
  - Why: a commodity producer can't innovate for customer value, because an
    innovative product falls outside the interchangeable pool and needs a new
    market, new distribution, and skills the producer has given up.
  - Failure mode: believing the firm, or separate teams within it, can pursue
    both. One viewpoint always dominates the culture.
- **60/45/20** (David C. Baker): hold utilization at 60% and payroll under 45%
  of AGI to earn a 20% margin.
  - When: the firm chooses efficiency and keeps tracking time.
  - How: utilization is hours billed divided by hours available; keep payroll at
    or below 45% of AGI.
  - Failure mode: it reliably lifts a firm to a profit threshold and then caps
    it there. Higher profit beyond it doesn't come from utilization.

## Concepts

- **Commodity**: an interchangeable, widely available, undifferentiated product
  that can't command a premium. The market sets the price; the producer only
  decides whether to sell.
- **Fungibility**: interchangeability. A brand appears the moment the buyer
  cares who the seller is.
- **Price taker vs. price maker**: the producer accepts the market's price; the
  marketer sets or negotiates it and earns the margins that pay for
  experimentation and expensive creative talent.
- **Culture**: the shared assumptions that steer what happens in an
  organization. It is why efficiency and innovation can't coexist.
- **Utilization**: hours billed as a share of hours available; the efficiency
  lever most creative firms reach for.
- **Innovation as experiment**: new offerings come from creative experiments
  tested in the market, and most fail. Failure and slack time are the opposite
  of efficiency.
- **Timesheets**: either the best efficiency tool or the surest way to kill
  innovation, depending on which path the firm chose. Tracking time is a choice
  about appetite for risk, not a moral question.

## Anti-patterns

- **Calling the profession commodified**: a great idea is the least commodified
  thing there is. Feeling commodified is a cultural choice that shows up as
  pricing pressure.
- **Chasing profit through utilization**: a contest to grow bigger and cheaper
  that does nothing for the price the firm can command.
- **Running an innovation team inside an efficiency culture**: the culture wins,
  and the innovators leave or conform.
- **Treating the trade-off as free**: the marketer path means more risk, more
  mess, and giving up metrics the firm has watched for years.

## Example

Enns's decision frame. Choose efficiency, and the firm keeps timesheets, runs
60/45/20, enjoys a large accessible market, and accepts whatever the market
decides it is worth. Choose innovation, and the firm becomes a price maker with
high margins but enters a contest of creativity and marketing, with more risk,
less certainty, and fewer familiar measures. Neither choice is immoral, and the
right one depends on appetite for risk, but only the second leads to the
high-margin firm the book is about.

## Takeaways

1. Decide explicitly whether the firm is a producer pursuing efficiency or a
   marketer pursuing value creation. Left alone, firms drift toward producer.
2. Expect every gain in utilization to cost some pricing power.
3. If the firm keeps timesheets, run 60/45/20, and notice when it becomes the
   ceiling.
4. Budget for failure: innovation needs time to think and experiments that don't
   pan out.
5. Sort pricing pressures into external (client, market) and internal (your own
   ideas of fairness). The internal ones are the easiest to remove.
