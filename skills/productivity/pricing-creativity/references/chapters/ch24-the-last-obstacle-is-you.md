## Core idea

Once every pricing technique is in place, the remaining gap between what a firm
earns and what it should earn is internal. Five mental barriers stand in the
way: low self-esteem, dysfunctional ideas about money, the labor theory of
value, confusing egalitarianism with fairness, and a scarcity mindset.

## Frameworks

- **Pricing performance: PP = M × A × SE** (motivation times ability times
  self-esteem), an extension of the classic performance formula of motivation
  times ability. Put bluntly, gross profit reflects many things, and the most
  important is the self-esteem of the person pricing and selling.
  - When: diagnosing chronic margin problems that persist despite sound pricing
    mechanics.
  - How to compensate (David C. Baker): confidence comes from (1) deeply held
    beliefs, starting with the belief that you are worthy, on which rest the
    beliefs that you are worth the price and that you, not the client, are the
    prize; and (2) options. When belief is weak, manage options: keep
    new-business opportunities above capacity (another reason to hold the client
    base near a dozen), keep the pipeline healthy and money in the bank, don't
    overinvest, and keep your desire for a deal slightly below the client's.
    Weak self-esteem calls for tighter control of everything else.
  - Baker's signs of low belief: discounting fees, modifying terms, accepting
    unusual invoicing procedures, giving advice before an engagement is agreed,
    and letting clients define the problem and treat the firm as a transactional
    fix.
- **Barrier: dysfunctional ideas about money.** Money triggers the brain's
  survival response. People uncomfortable with that fear blame money, spend only
  on necessities, yet hoard cash, despising it and clinging to it at once, and
  they resent anyone who asks others to part with more of it.
  - Reframe: money is what lets strangers cooperate, and it needs to keep
    circulating. The more money moves through your hands, the more everyone
    benefits. Any further judgment about money is projected fear.
- **Barrier: the labor theory of value.** Under stress, even people who believe
  in subjective value fall back on judging value by cost, asking how a price can
  be so high when the cost was so low.
  - Reframe: value, beauty, fairness, color, sound, and time are all constructs
    of the mind, not absolutes. Past the proof-of-concept stage, which does take
    hard work, success depends on creativity (seeing an opportunity) plus risk
    (leaping for it), which together make innovation, not labor. Pricing on
    effort condemns you to endless toil. When someone credits a successful
    person's hard work, Enns's reflex is to credit the risks they took instead.
- **Barrier: confusing egalitarianism with fairness** (Russ Roberts). Equal
  rights don't mean equal prices are fair. Fairness is a feeling, and you can't
  decide what others should feel.
  - Rule: you are responsible for a price that is fair to you and that you
    estimate gives the client a decent return. Whether the client feels it is
    fair is theirs to decide, and they will likely say so. Self-check: if you
    lean egalitarian by temperament (strengths assessments may show it), ask
    whether that has repeatedly led you to under- or overcharge.
- **Barrier: a scarcity mindset.** It values security over freedom and treats
  money, opportunity, and time as fixed, so every higher price looks greedy and
  business looks zero-sum, which Enns ranks among the most harmful beliefs a
  business owner can hold. Scarcity-minded people stop growing (Dan Sullivan),
  while abundance-minded people put their creative energy into building a better
  future.
  - Evidence for abundance: Peter Diamandis argues technology dematerializes,
    demonetizes, and democratizes; a phone replaces dozens of devices, and drink
    cans use far less material than they once did. Pine and Gilmore trace value
    from commodities to goods to services to experiences to transformations,
    each step worth roughly ten times the last: a birthday cake's ingredients
    cost cents, a mix a few dollars, a bakery cake tens of dollars, a staged
    party hundreds, and a transformation thousands, with almost none of the
    value tied to materials. Wealth now comes from intangibles: services, ideas,
    advice, knowledge, and experiences.
- **Now go get it**: entrepreneurs part with money easily because, seen from
  abundance, money is plentiful and can be earned again by helping others.
  Seeing trade as the way you help others clears the last barrier.

## Concepts

- **Self-esteem (SE)**: a subjective, emotional judgment of one's own worth; a
  multiplier in pricing performance. Gross profit can be read as a gauge of the
  pricer's self-esteem.
- **Options**: alternatives to the deal at hand; they stand in for confidence
  you don't yet feel.
- **The survival response to money**: fear triggered by financial threat,
  irrational but hardwired.
- **Labor theory of value**: value equals the cost to produce; the default to
  resist under stress. Wondering how a price can be so high when the work took
  so little time is the alarm that it has taken over.
- **Innovation as creativity plus risk**: the driver of success once an idea is
  proven.
- **Fairness as a feeling**: it can't be imposed on the client.
- **Zero-sum fallacy**: the belief that your gain is someone else's loss.
- **Dematerialization, demonetization, democratization**: Diamandis's three
  effects of technology.
- **Double thank-you**: a trade that leaves both parties better off and
  grateful.
- **Money as circulation**: treat money like blood that must keep moving, not a
  hoard.

## Anti-patterns

- **Pricing on what you think of your own abilities** rather than on what the
  market thinks of them, a pattern Baker saw in his clients.
- **Discounting, changing terms, odd invoicing, free advice, letting clients
  define the problem**: the behavioral signs of low belief.
- **The same price for everyone in the name of fairness**: the egalitarian
  confusion, and nobody agrees on what is fair anyway.
- **Hoarding cash while resenting money**: it bleeds into resenting your own
  prices.
- **Treating growth and higher prices as greed**: scarcity-driven self-sabotage.

## Example

David C. Baker's client firms kept chasing a price premium that never arrived.
His diagnosis was that they kept pricing on their own opinion of their abilities
rather than on the market's. The prescription: fill the pipeline beyond capacity
so every opportunity has an alternative, keep the roster near a dozen clients,
and bank cash. Then present the higher price with the confidence those options
give, while working on the underlying belief in your own worth.

## Takeaways

1. Audit yourself for Baker's signs of low belief; each one is a pricing leak.
2. Build confidence through options: a pipeline larger than capacity, about a
   dozen clients, cash reserves, and little overinvestment.
3. Notice labor-theory reflexes under stress, and price on risk and creativity,
   not effort.
4. Charge what is fair to you with a decent return for the client, and let the
   client judge fairness for themselves.
5. Reject zero-sum thinking; wealth is created by trading intangibles.
6. Relate to money as circulation, not survival.
