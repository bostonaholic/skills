# Pricing Creativity

Paraphrased, condensed frameworks from Blair Enns's book _Pricing Creativity: A
Guide to Profit Beyond the Billable Hour_. Credit the ideas to Enns and the
thinkers he cites. Dollar amounts are the book's examples and benchmarks; scale
them to the firm and client at hand.

## How to use

1. Name the task: setting a price, building options, preparing a value
   conversation, choosing a pricing model, answering a price objection,
   negotiating with procurement, or moving a firm off hourly billing.
2. Apply the core frameworks below. When a topic needs more depth, read the
   chapter the chapter index or topic index points to.
3. For a specific deal, gather the facts the rules depend on before pricing: the
   decision makers, the desired future state, what reaching it is worth, and the
   stated budget. Ask for whichever are missing.

## Core frameworks

### Value chain

Price before designing the work, in this order: Client, Value, Price, Cost,
Solutions. Learn who the client is and what the desired future state is worth to
them, set a price range, derive a cost ceiling (about 60% of the price), and
only then design the solution. Estimating hours first is the root mistake. Sell
value (the desired future state), not inputs (hours) or outputs (deliverables).
Value and fairness are subjective; fairness is how the client feels about the
price. The test of a maximal sustainable price is the double thank-you, where
both sides sincerely thank each other.

### The six rules

1. **Price the client, not the job.** Willingness to pay differs by client, so
   let those who can pay more do so. Never answer a request for the price of a
   service with a number. Publish guidance only, such as a minimum level of
   engagement (MLOE, about 10% of the annual AGI target), never per-service
   prices, unless the firm is productized.
2. **Offer options.** Three, four at most, with the highest price last on the
   page. Options change the client's question from whether the price is worth it
   to which option is the best value. Never add a cheaper option to play it
   safe.
3. **Anchor high.** Include an anchor priced above where the client will buy,
   and present it first. People adjust too little away from the first number
   they hear, and a high anchor causes no backlash. If the anchor sells 20% of
   the time or more, raise every price.
4. **Say a price before you show a price.** State the price or range aloud
   before it appears in writing, even for routine work with existing clients:
   call before sending the estimate. Early objections are cheap; late ones come
   wrapped in sunk cost. Enns's Rule of Money: firms that avoid talking about
   money don't make it.
5. **Master the value conversation.** See below.
6. **Limit unpaid proposals to one page.** Option columns and prices, nothing
   else, and never sent unattended. When scoping needs research, sell a paid
   diagnostic instead.

### Four conversations and The Flip

A sale is four conversations: probative, qualifying, value, and closing. Each
has one objective, and every one after the first also protects the firm's
position as an expert practitioner. The Flip is the moment the client starts
treating the firm as an expert rather than a vendor. It must happen before or
during the value conversation, or the proposal shrinks to rates and dates.

### The value conversation

Early in qualifying, ask Dan Sullivan's three-year question: the client imagines
meeting you three years from now, delighted with the progress since, and
describes what happened. Enns advises using Sullivan's exact wording. Then run
four steps:

1. **Commit the client to the desired future state.** Recap their organizational
   needs and personal wants, then confirm the recap is right and complete.
2. **Agree on metrics** for each item.
3. **Agree on the value of success**, in profit where possible. Quantify soft
   value only slightly past your comfort.
4. **Give pricing guidance** as a range from $X to $Y. The firm owns $Y and says
   it first, by anchoring against guaranteed value: ask whether a share of
   first-year profit (for example, a third) would be fair if the outcome were
   guaranteed. The client's budget is $X. If the client counters with a small
   share, ask whether they would really turn down an investment guaranteed to
   return several times its cost. Keep the guarantee hypothetical.

Never discuss or think about solutions during this conversation. Before leaving,
book the closing conversation with every decision maker.

### Building the options

- **Make the margin in the middle.** Design the middle option to be the one most
  clients choose and the one with the highest margin. The Home Depot Option,
  named for the retailer's do-it-yourself-with-our-help slogan, puts a
  diagnostic in the low option, full implementation in the anchor, and strategy
  plus help forms in the middle: knowledge transfer, oversight, standards,
  reporting, and open access.
- **Something for everyone.** The low option is a time block of junior hours
  with every other value driver stripped out, for the price buyer. The middle
  option holds the value drivers this client wants, for the poker player. The
  anchor comes from the money-no-object exercise. The structure works without
  diagnosing the buyer type.
- **A premium is insurance.** Prices sit on a risk spectrum from selling
  capacity or time (the client carries the risk, lowest price) to guaranteed
  value (the firm carries it, highest price). Name each option's risk when
  presenting, and always price one option above the stated budget. Guarantee
  client by client, never as policy, and carry only risk you can sleep on. Pair
  a satisfaction guarantee with a tip clause for a single executive buyer; with
  many stakeholders, use the tip clause alone.
- **Price integrity.** Strategic prices are round and end in three zeros. Use a
  charm price (for example, $19k instead of $20k) only to nudge toward the
  middle option.

### Pricing models

Most retainers are cost-based in disguise. Never build one by dividing the
annual budget by 12. The two sound uses are open access (paid, client-initiated
access to principals; Enns's floor was $5k a month, with 10% or more off for a
year paid in advance) and reserved capacity for large, sporadic implementation
needs. Lease software-like deliverables instead of selling them (a $50k website
became $3k a month for 36 months, $108k in total), protected by a
financed-amount clause. Decline points systems. For Agile work, fix the price of
discovery and tie the sprints to milestone incentives. Changing the pricing
model moves price more than haggling over the number does.

### Never discount

Discount only for loyal clients, and show it on the estimate and invoice as a
courtesy discount. Never discount to win a new client: the First-Job Rule says
the first job must be profitable. Before cutting price, match the objection.
Affordability gets payment terms, risk gets a guarantee, unwillingness gets
value removed, volume gets a trade for a larger written commitment, and anything
else gets a walk-away, which is usually a pause before returning with a new
structure.

### Negotiating

Enns's power formula is P = db ÷ D: power equals the client's desire for the
firm divided by the firm's desire for the client. The only real lever is a
credible willingness to walk, so don't overinvest in the pursuit. Procurement
that appears after the closing conversation means the firm has won: start with
no, out-wait the waiter, decide give-gets in advance, trial-close before
conceding, and neuter the final negotiators at the verbal yes by asking the
client to take procurement's call. Beware being the rabbit, a bidder invited
only to push another firm's price down, and the white knight, your own senior
person who gives away margin late. Invoke policy, because a predetermined
decision beats a preference, and remove pricing from the front lines: the person
who sets price should be the one least invested in the deal.

### Producer or marketer

A firm is either a producer (efficiency, timesheets, and the 60/45/20 targets
that become a ceiling) or a marketer (innovation, and the freedom to set its own
prices). Choose one. Hold about 10 to 12 client relationships as yield-managed
capacity, and grow by engagement size rather than client count. Track AGI/FTE,
not hours: about $160k is respectable (David C. Baker's benchmark), a gap just
above $200k marks the efficiency ceiling, and value pricers reach $250k or more.
To transition, give new clients new models and let old clients keep old prices.
Value-price strategy (diagnose, prescribe) first, and let commodified
re-application work go cheaper if the market insists.

### The last obstacle is you

PP = M × A × SE: pricing performance is motivation times ability times
self-esteem. The five mental barriers are low self-esteem, dysfunctional beliefs
about money, the labor theory of value (value equals effort), confusing equal
treatment with fairness, and a scarcity mindset. Make up for weak belief with
options: a pipeline larger than capacity, cash in the bank, and about a dozen
clients.
