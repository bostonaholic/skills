# Chapter 23: Begin to Untether From Time

Paraphrased summary of chapter 23 of Blair Enns's _Pricing Creativity_.

## Core idea

Stop selling time first, then stop tracking it. Both can happen in stages: new
clients before old, strategy before implementation, tracking time off before
dropping timesheets, firm-wide profit measures before job-level ones. Make up
for the lost job-level accuracy by charging more.

## Frameworks

- **Selling time, step 1: separate new clients from old.** A firm changes
  through the clients it signs from now on, not by converting the ones it has.
  - How: draw a line today. Every future client's first engagement is
    value-priced, with options and an anchor. Try converting existing clients
    only if you want to, and retreat without guilt if they resist. Old clients
    keep old prices and models; new clients get new ones.
  - Why: clients used to buying time resist changes to how they are billed, so
    conversions are a bonus, not the plan.
- **Selling time, step 2: value-price strategy first.** Work moves through four
  phases: diagnose, prescribe, apply, reapply. The first two cover strategy,
  planning, and diagnostics, and what the client buys there is thinking.
  - How: start by value-pricing strategy, even while implementation is still
    billed by the hour. Next bring creative concepts under value pricing, then
    the first application, which establishes the design language and is
    therefore worth more than the repeats that follow.
  - The tail: if re-application work is too commodified in the firm's market to
    value-price, don't fight it. Charge less for it, treating it as the
    commodity clients see it as, to raise the price of the strategy, but only if
    the strategy can be value-priced.
- **Tracking time, step 1: track time only for people who sell time.** If
  strategy isn't sold by the hour, free the strategy people from timesheets.
  Make some people hourly and others not, and free more over time.
  - Why: removing the principal's hourly rate removes the account team's
    incentive to keep clients away from the firm's senior thinkers. Take
    strategy off the top and account only for implementation in work in
    progress.
- **Tracking time, step 2: measure profit more broadly** (works well for smaller
  firms). Drop timesheets entirely and track one predictor of profitability,
  firm-wide profit or AGI/FTE (ch03), measured less often (quarterly) against a
  target. Timesheets come back only when firm-wide performance falls below
  target.
  - When someone asks how the firm will know whether each job is profitable: it
    won't, and it will still misprice some work. Value pricing gives up the
    right to bill the client for the firm's own bad scoping, so charge more to
    compensate.
- **Track time off, not time**: told he would earn far more by tracking his
  time, Enns did the opposite. He set a target for days off (six weeks) and
  tracked only that. He ended up taking more than nine weeks off with the same
  income and none of the burnout that had repeatedly put him in bed.
- **Two stages of success** (Dan Sullivan): the first level comes from hard work
  and saying yes; the second requires innovation and saying no, and giving up
  the first-level tools. Innovation is wasteful by nature and needs freedom to
  fail, and saying no funds that time. Warren Buffett makes a similar point
  about how much successful people decline.
- **A last lesson from car dealers**: dealers charge friends and family more,
  about 25% more gross, because they get better service (the front of the repair
  line, the salesperson's car as a loaner), and the dealers would resent them
  otherwise. Apply it: messy or demanding relationships need different
  economics, so charge more to cover the mess rather than tracking every job.

## Concepts

- **Efficiency paradigm**: the mindset of selling and tracking time, which
  blocks innovation and outsized profit.
- **Diagnose, prescribe, apply, reapply**: the four phases of a client
  engagement. Value-price from the strategy end and move toward the application
  end as confidence grows.
- **Commodified re-application**: the repeat-execution phase that clients see as
  a commodity.
- **AGI/FTE**: AGI per full-time equivalent employee, a firm-level predictor of
  profit.
- **Conditional timesheets**: used only when the firm falls below its target,
  like an alarm rather than a dashboard.
- **Time as a construct**: like value and fairness, time exists in the observer
  rather than as an absolute (Enns draws on Einstein).
- **Second-level tools**: innovation, risk-taking, and saying no.

## Anti-patterns

- **Switching all clients at once**: resistance from old clients derails the
  transition.
- **Insisting on value-pricing commodified re-application**: a fight the firm
  may not win; discount it instead to raise the strategy price.
- **Keeping the principal on an hourly rate**: rewards hiding senior talent from
  clients.
- **Obsessing over job-level profit under value pricing**: efficiency culture at
  the expense of value, and overruns can't be billed anyway.
- **Saying yes to everything at the second level**: what got the firm here won't
  get it there.

## Example

Enns, then a solo consultant repeatedly sick from overwork, was told he would
earn far more by tracking his time. He reversed it: he set a target of six weeks
off for the next year and tracked only that. The result was more than nine weeks
off, the same income, and no burnout. The firm-level version replaces timesheets
with a quarterly AGI/FTE target and brings timesheets back only if the target is
missed.

## Takeaways

1. Put every client signed from now on onto value pricing, and count any
   converted existing client as a bonus.
2. Move strategy to value pricing first, concepts second, and the first
   application third, cutting the price of commodified repeat work if necessary.
3. Free people who don't sell hours from timesheets now, and widen the list over
   time.
4. Swap per-job time tracking for a single quarterly firm metric, profit or
   AGI/FTE, and bring timesheets back only when it misses.
5. Charge more to cover messiness and mispricing, since value pricing means
   overruns can't be billed.
6. Track time off, not time, and say no to fund innovation.
