## Core idea

The anchor comes from asking what the firm would offer, and at what price, to a
client with no budget limit, then reconciling that concierge option with the
guaranteed-value number from the value conversation, with the uncertainty
discount factored back in. A fully guaranteed option is legitimate but rarely
makes the page, and such bets should be managed as a portfolio.

## Frameworks

- **Money-no-object exercise**: for this specific engagement, ask what the firm
  would do and charge for a client who would follow every recommendation and pay
  whatever it required.
  - When: building the anchor for every proposal.
  - How:
    1. Let go of every belief about what this client can afford.
    2. Revisit the value driver inventory (ch04) beyond the core service and
       pick the drivers that belong in a concierge-level option.
    3. Price it. Even four times the client's budget (for example $60k against
       $15k) is fine.
    4. Present it first, framed as a thought experiment: before thinking about
       solutions, the team asked what it would do with no budget constraint, and
       it wants to share the result because it may spark ideas.
    5. Leave its value items in view while walking down to the cheaper options.
       Expect requests for the middle option plus a couple of items from the
       anchor; with collaborative clients that is a healthy starting point for
       negotiation (ch12 covers when to hold firm).
  - Why: anchoring (ch07) plus exposure. Expense-minded people gravitate to the
    cheapest option, but others in the room start wanting pieces of the
    expensive one.
- **Factoring the uncertainty discount back in**: start from the fee the client
  called fair for a guaranteed result in the value conversation (for example $1M
  against a guaranteed $3M a year), then estimate what the concierge option is
  worth to them with no guarantee attached. The result moves the anchor price
  closer to final without necessarily settling it.
- **Contingency and performance pay**: offer a guaranteed anchor only when the
  firm is truly prepared to stand behind the outcome. Enns opposes blanket
  pay-for-performance schemes; following Rule #1, the choice is made separately
  for each client.
- **Client compensation portfolio** (Ron Baker): manage clients the way an
  investor manages holdings, blending a few risky, lucrative engagements with
  safer, modest ones until the overall risk matches what the firm can stomach,
  so it stays profitable without losing sleep. Rebalance through the options in
  the next few proposals. Baker pairs this with airline seating (first class,
  business, premium economy, economy, deep discount), with one difference: the
  firm's first-class seats carry the firm's own risk, so an all-first-class
  airline means sleepless nights.

## Concepts

- **Anchor**: the highest-priced option, priced through the money-no-object
  exercise and adjusted for uncertainty.
- **Guaranteed-value anchor**: the theoretical maximum from the value
  conversation; it becomes a real option only with a real guarantee.
- **Contingency fee**: the seller takes all the performance risk for an
  all-or-nothing payoff. Outside litigation, most such deals leave the seller
  with nothing.
- **Performance pay**: payment at least partly tied to results; an option for a
  particular client, not a firm-wide rule.
- **Uncertainty discount**: the reduction from the guaranteed price to the price
  without a guarantee, for the same outcome.
- **Concierge option**: the full-value option unconstrained by budget.
- **Risk profile**: the firm's comfortable average across high- and low-risk
  engagements.
- **Ceiling and discount**: the guaranteed price is the ceiling, and the
  concierge price is that ceiling minus what uncertainty is worth to the client.

## Anti-patterns

- **Pricing every engagement on performance**: an all-first-class airline in
  which every seat carries the firm's risk.
- **Dismissing guaranteed options outright**: they are legitimate anchors and
  occasionally a windfall.
- **Letting beliefs about the client's budget cap the anchor**: defeats the
  exercise, since the anchor isn't meant to be bought.
- **Taking a contingency deal without portfolio balance**: all-or-nothing deals
  usually end with the seller unpaid. Enns's own limit is one very high-risk
  client at a time.

## Example

A consultant emailed a novel fundraising idea to a referral whose venture needed
$2.5M. Instead of signing off with good wishes, he named a fee of $600,000 for
the idea and for helping implement it, and the client agreed immediately. The
logic: the idea would raise about $3.1M, and the consultant kept the value
created above the $2.5M goal. It was pure contingency, with years of part-time
work and travel (expenses covered); the scope and commission grew, and it paid
off. Enns treats it as the exception, balanced by the consultant's
conventionally priced, risk-free work.

## Takeaways

1. Build the anchor by pricing what the firm would do for a client with no
   budget limit; ignore this client's stated budget.
2. Present it first, as a thought experiment that might spark ideas.
3. Work down from the guaranteed-value figure, discounting for uncertainty, to
   reach the anchor price.
4. Include a guaranteed or contingency option only when the firm will actually
   guarantee, and decide per client, not as policy.
5. Keep the roster balanced: a few high-risk bets on a base of low-risk
   engagements, rebalanced through the next proposals.
