## Core idea

Value pricing succeeds or fails in one conversation: the one that commits the
client to a desired future state, puts a number on what reaching it is worth,
and gives a price range, high end first, all without mentioning solutions. It is
tacit knowledge, learned only by doing, and most people quit after one or two
clumsy attempts.

## Frameworks

- **Just Four Conversations**: the arc of a sale. Each conversation has one
  objective and a framework, and every one after the first also has to maintain
  the firm's practitioner position.
  1. **Probative**: prove expertise, so the client moves the firm from vendor to
     expert practitioner in their mind. Ideally this happens without you,
     through thought leadership and referrals.
  2. **Qualifying**: vet the lead (need or desired future state, timing,
     authority, budget) and decide next steps. Do it person to person, never by
     email. Move on to the value conversation when the client has a need that
     matches the firm's expertise and an intent to act.
  3. **Value**: work out the value the firm might create and the share of it the
     firm might command.
  4. **Closing**: move smoothly from sale to engagement by helping the client
     choose among options. Trouble here almost always starts in an earlier
     conversation.
- **Three preconditions for an easy value conversation**:
  1. The client sees the firm as the expert. Watch for The Flip, when the client
     picks up a pen or defers to you on process and asks what happens next. If
     it hasn't happened, it has to happen during the value conversation, or the
     proposal shrinks to rates and dates.
  2. The firm has uncovered the client's desired future state, in qualifying or
     at the latest at the start of the value conversation.
  3. Senior decision makers responsible for future value are present. Staff
     focus on the past, managers on the present, and only executives on future
     value. Senior people show up for magic (creative, transformative work), not
     logic (operational, efficiency work).
- **Three-year question** (Dan Sullivan): ask the client to imagine the two of
  you meeting three years from now, with the client delighted by the progress
  made since, and to describe what happened to make them so happy.
  - Enns says to use Sullivan's exact wording and warns against editing it; the
    original is in Sullivan's _The Dan Sullivan Question_ and in this chapter of
    the book.
  - When: as early as possible in qualifying. It replaces repeated why-questions
    for peeling back need. It draws out personal wants, which outrank
    organizational needs, often triggers The Flip, and supplies the raw material
    for step one. Take detailed notes and probe.
- **Four-step value conversation**:
  1. **Commit the client to the desired future state.** Recap what the
     three-year question surfaced: the organizational needs and the personal
     wants, using discretion about which personal wants to repeat in front of
     colleagues. Then ask whether the recap is right and whether anything is
     missing. This shows you listened, missed nothing, and are focused on them.
  2. **Agree on the metrics of success.** For each item in the recap, ask for a
     measurable goal: how everyone will know the brand has moved upmarket, what
     the specific revenue target is, how much of the client's own time the
     project should take at most. Senior decision makers especially value this
     step.
  3. **Agree on the value of success.** For each metric, ask what achieving it
     is worth. Do the math for the client and convert revenue to profit where
     possible. Quantify non-economic value too, but push only slightly past your
     comfort; turning emotional value into dollars too eagerly alienates
     clients. Total the easy math, then round up modestly for the murkier value,
     an intuitive discount for uncertainty.
  4. **Give pricing guidance.** Say the firm will come back with options ranging
     from $X to $Y. This foreshadows the proposal so nothing surprises the
     client, primes them to think in ROI rather than expense, and reveals their
     willingness to pay, so objections get handled now and options get built
     with that knowledge. Most sales advice omits this step, and it matters most
     when the value and closing conversations are separated in time.
- **Setting the range**: the firm supplies $Y, the high end, said first per Rule
  #3. The client supplies $X, their budget. Any price must give the client a
  fair return by their own definition and reflect uncertainty about the firm's
  ability to deliver.
  - Benchmarks for $Y: large consultancies charge about half of first-year
    savings; Alan Weiss starts at 20% of the value created (a 5:1 return) and
    adjusts.
  - **Anchoring against guaranteed value**: hypothetically remove all
    uncertainty. The weak form asks what fair compensation would be if the firm
    could guarantee every outcome discussed. The strong form primes with a
    number: it asks whether a third of the first year's new profit (for example,
    a third of $3M) would be fair if the firm guaranteed that result.
  - If the client counters low (say 10%, or $300k), turn it around: ask whether
    they would really refuse to spend $1M for a guaranteed $3M of profit every
    year for several years. That flushes out the real objection, which is
    whether the firm will guarantee anything.
  - If the client asks whether the firm will really guarantee it, keep the door
    open but the question hypothetical: you are open to it, and you mainly
    wanted their sense of a fair return.
  - **Uncertainty discount**: the guaranteed price is the ceiling, and every
    option without a guarantee is discounted for uncertainty, above all
    performance risk. The client's stated budget is their own
    uncertainty-discounted number; treat it as $X, not as a rejection. The lower
    the uncertainty of the outcome, the higher the price.
- **Closing the conversation**: explain that the team will consider the
  variables and the ways it could help create the value discussed, then return
  with options. Give the range out loud, for example about $1M at the top for a
  guaranteed solution, with options below it down to what the firm can do for
  $100k.
- **Final steps**: book the closing conversation before leaving, with the
  calendar open now. It is not a closing conversation unless every necessary
  decision maker attends, so confirm who must be there.

## Concepts

- **The Flip**: the moment the client's view of the firm moves from vendor to
  expert; the sale gets easier from there.
- **Negotiating status**: the firm's standing to have a peer-level conversation.
  Vendors get talked down to and have terms dictated.
- **Desired future state**: what the client wants, personally and
  organizationally; what the firm is really selling and pricing.
- **Magic vs. logic** (Charles Kirchner): magic is creative work driven ad hoc
  by a few talented people; logic is factory-style efficiency. Senior clients
  attend for magic.
- **Anchoring against guaranteed value**: asking what the client would pay for a
  guaranteed outcome to find their theoretical maximum, $Y.
- **Performance risk**: doubt about the firm's ability to deliver the quantified
  value; the biggest discount on any price.
- **Value chain**: Client, Value, Price, Cost, Solutions. The firm leaves the
  value conversation without having thought about solutions.
- **The budget as a bet**: a low budget is the client's risk-adjusted bid. A
  large budget is a safe but costly bet and a small one a cheap but risky bet,
  and either can cost the client their job.

## Anti-patterns

- **Talking solutions in the value conversation**: competitors sell solutions to
  organizational needs; the firm stays fixed on the client's future state and
  does not even think about solutions.
- **Holding a value conversation with someone who owns no future value**: it
  goes nowhere. Get executives in the room.
- **Editing the three-year question**: it is engineered, and changes weaken it.
- **Skipping step four**: leads to surprised clients and unhandled objections at
  the closing conversation.
- **Over-monetizing emotional value**: feels mercenary; stop just past your
  comfort.
- **Quitting after an early stumble**: the most common reason firms abandon
  value pricing. Keep failing forward.
- **Leaving without a booked closing conversation that includes every decision
  maker.**

## Example

A brand repositioning client.

- **Step 1**: the recap covers moving from a sub-premium to a leading premium
  brand, record revenue, needing little hand-holding of the firm, proactive
  ideas beyond the brief, telling the story internally before relaunch, and
  early, provable ROI for the CFO. The client confirms it is right and complete.
- **Step 2**: gross margin from 11% to 18%, sales from $20M to $25M, and a cap
  on the client's own time.
- **Step 3**, worked aloud: 7 points of margin on $20M is $1.4M; $5M of new
  sales at 18% is $0.9M; together about $2.3M a year in profit, rounded up for
  the murkier items to more than $3M.
- **Step 4**: the firm asks whether a fee of a third of year-one profit would be
  fair if the firm guaranteed $3M of new annual profit. The client counters with
  10%, or $300k. The firm asks whether the client would really refuse to spend
  $1M for a guaranteed $3M of profit every year. The client says only with an
  ironclad guarantee; the firm says it is open to that but mainly wanted the
  client's view of a fair return. The client reveals a $100k budget.
- **Result**: $X is $100k and $Y is $1M. The firm gives the closing summary,
  books the closing conversation, and never discusses solutions.

## Takeaways

1. Know which of the four conversations you are in and its single objective, and
   never give up the practitioner position.
2. Ask the three-year question early, in Sullivan's wording, and take notes. It
   replaces repeated why-questions and helps trigger The Flip.
3. Run the four steps in order: commit, metrics, value, pricing guidance. The
   client is paying for their future state, discounted for uncertainty.
4. Do the client's math for them, in profit, then round up modestly for what
   can't be quantified.
5. Say $Y first by anchoring against guaranteed value with a concrete share, and
   let the client's budget be $X.
6. Neither offer nor refuse a guarantee in the room. Keep it hypothetical and
   decide while building options.
7. Leave with the closing conversation on the calendar and every decision maker
   confirmed. Skill comes only from repetition.
