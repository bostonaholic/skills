# Chapter 1: What Price for a Logo?

Paraphrased summary of the introduction to Blair Enns's _Pricing Creativity_.

## Core idea

Value and fairness are subjective, even between businesses. The price of
creative or advisory work depends on the client's circumstances (the risk they
face, the prestige at stake, the peace of mind they want), not on hours, effort,
or aesthetic quality. Creative firms are held back by pricing conventions they
carry in their own heads more than by the market. Change how you charge first,
then how much.

## Frameworks

- **Value triad** (Macdivitt and Wilkinson): value is revenue gains plus cost
  reductions plus emotional contributions.
  - When: a client seems to judge the firm on something other than pure ROI, or
    you need to name what the client is really paying for.
  - How: estimate the economic outcome (revenue gained, cost avoided), then list
    the emotional contributions for each decision maker: prestige, risk
    avoidance, simplicity, peace of mind, self-esteem, personal gain, time
    saved. Treat the emotional contributions as multipliers of the economic
    value, not additions to it.
  - Why: without emotional value, the lowest bidder always wins. With it, bids
    stop being directly comparable and a premium becomes possible.
  - Failure mode: assuming every member of a client committee values the deal
    the same way. They rarely do, even with shared corporate goals.
- **Double thank-you**: the deal closes with each side sincerely thanking the
  other.
  - When: as the test of a maximal, sustainable price.
  - How: create as much value as possible, communicate it, and capture as much
    of it as you can while both thank-yous stay sincere.
  - Why: a client who feels the deal was good enough to repeat gladly becomes
    repeat business at higher prices.
- **Small price rise, large profit gain**: in most firms, raising prices 10%
  adds about 50% to profit, and some firms see profit double or more in the
  first year.

## Concepts

- **Subjective theory of value**: value is not inside the thing; it exists in
  the person valuing it. It displaced the labor theory of value in economics in
  the 1870s.
- **Labor theory of value**: value equals the labor needed to produce something.
  Hourly billing is its modern descendant.
- **Creations of the mind**: ideas, advice, and designs. They are among the
  least commodified things there are, yet the people who sell them often feel
  commodified.
- **Risk mitigation**: the most powerful emotional contribution to value.
- **Fairness**: a feeling about the price, not a calculation of it. Pricing
  strategist Reed Holden makes this point. Lowering a price the client already
  feels is fair imposes your valuation on them.
- **Fairness by history**: resistance to raising prices usually comes from what
  you charged before, not from what is fair.
- **Price communication**: in Uber's surge-pricing research, an odd multiplier
  (2.1x) felt fairer to riders than a round one (2.0x). Every price increase
  needs communication around it.

## Anti-patterns

- **Answering what a logo costs with a number**: the honest answer is that it
  depends on the client's circumstances, and quality and hours are not the
  circumstances that matter.
- **Counting hours on a risk-laden engagement**: the client is buying protection
  from a costly failure, not your time.
- **Long deck proposals**: bios, free diagnosis, and an itemized price on the
  last page invite attack. A one-page proposal with three options does not.
- **Small, rare, nervous increases**: each modest rise becomes the new reference
  point. Raise prices more, and more often.
- **Self-imposed margin caps**: treating margins above some level as unfair to
  the client caps the upside. Margin benchmarks (15% to 20% respectable, 30%
  ambitious) are floors, not ceilings.

## Examples

- **The three-column proposal.** A Toronto UX firm sent a one-page proposal with
  three columns priced at $250k, $400k, and $600k. The client asked for the
  middle option plus a few items from the top one, and they agreed at $475k.
  Nobody asked how the round numbers were derived. Enns traces the technique to
  Tim Williams, and through him to Ron Baker. It confirmed two of Enns's
  convictions: selling hours won't make you rich, and you never justify a price.
- **Nike and Pepsi.** In 1971 Nike paid a student $35 for the swoosh. In 2008
  PepsiCo paid a design agency about $1M for a logo redesign, thousands of times
  more for work that was neither thousands of times better nor much slower to
  make. The gap is risk: the same agency's Tropicana packaging redesign soon cut
  sales by more than 20% and cost about $30M within two months, and a comparable
  Pepsi failure would have cost far more. By that logic Pepsi arguably paid too
  little.
- **Twenty minutes, $3,500.** Enns priced a small engagement at $3,500 based on
  the result the client wanted and delivered it in 20 minutes. Both sides were
  delighted and thanked each other, though the implied hourly rate passed
  $10,000.

## Takeaways

1. Price from the client's economic outcome, magnified by their emotional stakes
   (especially risk), never from hours or effort.
2. Expect each decision maker to value the same proposal differently; find the
   one whose job or prestige is on the line.
3. Aim for the double thank-you, not a margin benchmark.
4. Pair every price increase with communication. Fairness is a feeling to
   manage, not a number to justify.
5. When a new price feels unfair to you, recognize that as your history talking.
6. Change how you charge before changing how much; that is what turns a 10%
   price move into much higher profit.
