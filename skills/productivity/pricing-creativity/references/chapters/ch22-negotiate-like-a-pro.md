## Core idea

The one-page, three-option proposal does most of the negotiating. Nine tips
handle the late arrivals (procurement, legal, accounting), who are trained to
keep asking until they hear no and who lose if the firm holds onto its power.

## Frameworks

- **Don't overinvest**: power follows the formula P = db ÷ D, where P is the
  firm's power in the sale, db is the firm's desirability (how much the client
  wants the firm), and D is the firm's own desire for the client. Whoever wants
  the deal more has less power and bears most of the cost of working out the
  fit.
  - Three sunk costs that signal neediness: time and effort (the all-night,
    50-page deck), emotion (cheerful follow-ups to rude silence), and money
    (flights, hired researchers, staff pulled off paying work).
  - How: keep a professional, clinical demeanor; human, but not eager to please.
    When messages go unanswered, point out the silence and ask what that
    suggests about working together.
  - Why: the only real lever is walking away, or the client believing the firm
    might. Sunk costs make that impossible.
- **Stop negotiating when you've won**: if procurement appears only after the
  closing conversation, the firm has almost certainly won, and it isn't in
  procurement's interest to say so. Tom Kinnaird, a former procurement executive
  at WPP, says procurement routinely invents a shortlist of rival firms to
  pressure the one it has already chosen.
  - How: start with no. If you must concede, trade price for reduced value.
    Before negotiating, ask whether you've already won.
  - If procurement arrives early, price will decide everything; think hard about
    taking part at all. A firm that hears it is being bought as a commodity can
    reply that the client has called the wrong firm.
  - Further reading: Gerry Preece, _Buying Less for Less_.
- **Out-wait the waiter**: procurement stalls (days of silence, then a 24-hour
  demand) to test how badly the firm wants the deal.
  - How: slow down further. If they take a day, take two; if they are out next
    week, so is the firm, plus a couple of days. Send the business client a
    courtesy note that the firm will fit in another project while procurement is
    busy and will be ready when they are.
  - Goal: in this three-way game, the client must be the most anxious party, so
    the firm can out-stall procurement. When the client calls to ask what is
    happening, explain that procurement is stalling, remind them of the
    agreement, and offer to agree on a small concession procurement can claim
    that isn't price or terms.
- **Decide concessions in advance (give-gets)**: like a bidder who sets an
  auction limit beforehand, decide what the firm will give and what it will get
  in return (for example, accepting 60-day payment terms instead of 30 to hold
  the price).
  - Caveat: having decided on a trade, don't offer it too early.
- **Neuter the final negotiators**: the firm's power peaks just after the verbal
  agreement; use it to head off procurement.
  - How: at the end of the closing conversation, predict procurement's call.
    Explain that procurement will ask for a lower price, that both sides already
    agreed the price is fair, so the answer will be no, and that the paperwork
    may then stall for weeks on a project that could start next week. Then ask,
    with a smile, whether you may refer procurement to the client when the call
    comes.
  - Works best with senior clients who can overrule procurement. When
    procurement calls, the firm can say it expected the call and that the
    client's chief executive would like a word with them about it.
- **Use alternative forms of no**: no is the second-best answer after yes, and
  it usually means not now.
  - **Positive no**: restate the ask, then say yes to the underlying need rather
    than the specific request. If the client says they can't afford it, agree
    that affordability is the issue and offer to stretch the payment terms,
    asking what terms they need. Everything about your manner says yes.
  - **Ask how** (Chris Voss): when a client wants a $75k option for $50k, ask
    how you would do that. If they say just cut $25k, ask whether they think the
    firm has a third of the price in margin, or whether it should work at a
    loss. Then think aloud about handing the work to the most junior team. Keep
    this for outlandish asks.
  - **Yes, but**: agree the firm can meet the budget, then list what that means:
    the junior team, no access to principals, payment up front. This separates
    price buyers from poker players, since a value buyer will refuse those
    terms.
- **Don't negotiate against yourself**: a request for just one thing more
  (60-day terms instead of 30) is usually the first item of a list delivered one
  at a time, like the television detective with one more question.
  - How: trial close with an if-then. Ask whether, if the firm agreed to 60-day
    terms, the two sides would have an agreement or whether something else
    stands in the way. If the answer is yes, stay cautious: ask for the
    agreement and say you will take it to your team or CFO to get it signed.
- **Beware the rabbit**: the rabbit is a low-priced firm invited to bid only to
  drive another firm's price down; it is never seriously considered. A claim
  that the firm's bid is the highest is often untrue, and late quotes are
  gathered as leverage.
  - When told that policy requires three bids once the firm gives its best
    offer: invite them to get the three bids, and to come back if they still
    want to talk; if they find a better fit, no hard feelings.
  - Earlier in the sale: say the firm doesn't do bidding wars, so they should
    collect their bids and then talk. The reaction shows whether the firm is the
    expert practitioner or the rabbit. If the opportunity seemed too good to be
    true, the firm may be the rabbit.
- **Beware the white knight**: a senior person on the firm's side who swoops in
  late to save the deal and gives away the margin others fought for, which
  procurement reads as an invitation to ask for more.
  - How: keep discount-prone rescuers out of the negotiation; senior people
    attend only when the client's senior people do. Watch for indirect pressure,
    such as constant requests for status, and ask yourself whether you are the
    white knight.

## Concepts

- **P = db ÷ D**: power in the sale is the firm's desirability divided by its
  own desire.
- **Sunk cost bias**: investment that makes walking away harder (ch18).
- **Give-gets**: concession pairs decided in advance.
- **Trial close**: an if-then question that tests whether a concession would end
  the negotiation.
- **Rabbit**: a low bidder used as procurement's lever.
- **White knight**: a senior insider who leaks margin at the finish.
- **One more thing**: serial single asks, like the car dealer's security bump
  (ch21).
- **The client as ally**: late procurement is confirmation of a win, not a
  competition, and the client is the firm's ally against its own bureaucracy.

## Anti-patterns

- **Answering serial concessions one at a time**: negotiating against yourself.
- **Matching procurement's urgency**: hands over power; out-wait them instead.
- **Offering pre-decided trades early**: wastes the give-get.
- **Bidding as the rabbit**: the firm is a pawn, not a contender.
- **A principal jumping in to close**: a big late concession invites more.

## Example

The client gives a verbal yes on the $75k option. The firm predicts
procurement's call, explains it will say no to a price cut, and warns that the
paperwork may then stall for weeks on work that could start next Monday. It asks
whether it may refer procurement to the client, and the client laughs and
agrees. Procurement calls, says the field is down to three, and asks for a
sharper price. The firm says it expected the call and that the client's chief
executive would like a word with procurement about it. Procurement then asks for
60-day terms as its one thing. The firm asks whether agreeing to 60 days would
settle everything or whether something else stands in the way. The contract
arrives, the firm takes it to its CFO, and it signs.

## Takeaways

1. Know the firm's power: want the deal less than the client does, and cut
   visible sunk costs.
2. Procurement after the closing conversation means the firm has won; start with
   no.
3. Out-wait the waiter, and keep the client the most anxious party.
4. Decide give-gets in advance, and don't offer them early.
5. Head off procurement at the moment of verbal agreement.
6. Say no as a positive no, by asking how, or with a yes, but.
7. Trial-close every small extra ask, refuse to be the rabbit, and bench the
   white knight.
