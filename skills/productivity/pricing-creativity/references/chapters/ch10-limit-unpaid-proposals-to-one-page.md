# Chapter 10: Rule #6, Limit Unpaid Proposals to One Page

## Core idea

The proposal is what the firm says in the closing conversation. The written
document is a single page of three or four priced columns that supports that
conversation. Anything that needs real discovery to scope is a paid proposal,
sold as a diagnostic.

## Frameworks

- **Rule #6, limit unpaid proposals to one page**: any proposal written without
  pay is one page, with options in columns and prices at the bottom.
  - When: the solutions are obvious and the costs easy to estimate, as with
    routine engagements that vary little between clients, or clients the firm
    knows deeply.
  - How: build the page from the value chain: price first, then the cost worth
    incurring at each price, then the solution that fits. Present it in
    conversation; never send it unattended.
  - Failure mode: treating the proposal as a persuasion tool or a contract. That
    produces bloated documents and decks that work without you, which strips the
    firm's power in the sale.
- **Paid vs. unpaid proposals**: the dividing line is how much discovery scoping
  needs. If the firm must run audits, interviews, or other research to scope and
  price, it gets paid to write the proposal.
- **Diagnostic**: a branded, formal discovery process, sold as the paid first
  phase of an engagement.
  - Output: findings and recommendations (what the firm found and what the
    client should do), plus the one-page proposal of options for implementation.
  - When to sell it: usually at the end of the value conversation, right after
    pricing guidance, positioned as the small paid first step that narrows the
    $X to $Y range to the right solution. Sometimes earlier, at the end of
    qualifying, when the client admits a problem they don't understand (losing
    share to a weaker competitor without knowing why) or an unclear solution.
  - Rules: a diagnostic is the exception to Rule #2, so recommend a single one;
    many firms keep a standard diagnostic and adjust it slightly per client.
    Tell the client from the start that the findings will arrive with priced
    options for what comes next. When the diagnostic is sold during qualifying,
    the value conversation moves inside it rather than disappearing. Treat the
    findings presentation as a closing conversation, with all decision makers
    present and the one-page options as the focus.
- **Order from sale to contract** (simple, hard to argue with, rarely followed):
  1. Options with prices, delivered in conversation and supported by one page.
  2. Agreement in principle on the option, price, and start date.
  3. A contract covering the remaining details and any back-and-forth.
  4. The signed contract and a check or purchase order.
- **Setting up the closing conversation**: decline requests to just send the
  proposal. Explain that the written proposal is short, only a summary of
  options that make sense with the explanation, and that you don't want anyone
  reading it without that context. As a policy, the firm doesn't send proposals
  out unattended.
- **Facilitate, don't close**: in the closing conversation, restate why everyone
  is there (the desired future state, the metrics, the value), walk the options
  from high to low explaining the trade-offs, then ask. When the group
  converges, confirm the choice and move straight to the paperwork and deposit.

## Concepts

- **Unpaid proposal**: one page of three or four columns, prices running low to
  high; needs no discovery beyond the sale itself.
- **Paid proposal**: the same single page, preceded by a diagnostic report of
  findings and recommendations.
- **Diagnostic**: branded, paid discovery. Higher-priced firms almost always
  start engagements this way.
- **The proposal is spoken**: the firm says it has a few ways to help at
  different prices and then explains them.
- **The document as prop**: brief visual support. The more detail it holds, the
  further it can travel without you, and the less power the firm keeps.
- **Agreement in principle**: the client has chosen the option and price before
  any contract is drafted.
- **Closing conversation**: any meeting where the client chooses among options,
  including the presentation of diagnostic findings; every decision maker
  attends.

## Anti-patterns

- **Padding the proposal with firm history, awards, bios, photos, or sales
  copy**: a crutch to avoid asking for the decision.
- **Free diagnostic findings or strategic recommendations in the proposal**:
  that gives away the paid diagnostic.
- **Terms, conditions, and signature blocks in the proposal**: they belong in
  the contract, after agreement in principle.
- **Turning the proposal into a slide deck**: Enns dismisses the whole practice.
- **Sending proposals unattended**: removes the conversation the document
  depends on.
- **Offering several diagnostic options**: Rule #2 doesn't apply; propose the
  right one.
- **Presenting diagnostic findings without the decision makers**: it is a
  closing conversation, so treat it as one.

## Example

A closing conversation. Open by restating the client's desired future state, the
metrics, and the $3M a year of value agreed in the value conversation. Walk the
one-page proposal from the anchor down to the low option, naming each trade-off.
Ask which option makes the most sense; when the client picks the middle option,
promise the contract the same day. The contract carries the terms and
signatures, and the proposal carried no sales pitch and no office photos.

## Takeaways

1. If discovery wasn't paid for, the proposal is one page: three or four
   columns, prices at the bottom, nothing else.
2. Work from price to cost to solution; decide what each column costs only after
   its price is set.
3. When scoping requires research, sell a single diagnostic without options, and
   promise priced implementation options when the findings are presented.
4. Present every proposal yourself, in person or on a call; don't let it travel
   alone.
5. Keep terms and signature lines for the contract, and draft the contract only
   once the client has agreed in principle.
6. Run the closing conversation as a facilitator: recap the value, move from the
   most expensive option down, ask for a choice, and begin the paperwork.
7. Resist the urge to fatten a proposal up, and sell a diagnostic instead of
   guessing at scope.
