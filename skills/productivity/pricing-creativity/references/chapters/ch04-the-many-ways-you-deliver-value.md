## Core idea

A firm delivers value in many more forms than its core service, and different
clients value those forms differently. Like an airline, bundle them into whole
offers (never sell them item by item), price in round numbers, and treat the
firm's capacity of about 10 to 12 client relationships as a hard limit to be
yield-managed.

## Frameworks

- **Value driver inventory** (the airline lesson): list every form of value the
  firm delivers. These are the building blocks of every proposal.
  - When: before assembling options, or when you suspect the firm overvalues its
    core service and assumes everyone should pay about the same for it.
  - How: write the firm's own list. Enns's list for a design firm: design;
    design quality and creativity; the firm's prestige; speed and turnaround;
    reserved capacity; research; prototyping; testing; account and project
    management; reporting (written, by phone, in person); involvement of and
    access to senior people; emergency access on evenings and weekends;
    presentations to staff, management, or the board; knowledge transfer
    (training, workshops, videos, manuals); oversight of the client's in-house
    team; standards guides; access to the firm's supplier network; price
    certainty through a fixed price; payment terms; guarantees.
  - Why: one-way fares on a single route can vary about twentyfold for the same
    trip, because passengers buy different bundles of timing, certainty,
    comfort, and status, and accept that the person in the next seat paid a
    different price.
- **Bundle, don't unbundle**: never let clients buy value drivers one at a time.
  - How: assemble drivers into whole options. Keep offers easy to understand and
    hard to compare, and vary the bundles so competitors can't match them line
    by line.
  - Why: item-by-item pricing invites direct comparison, pushes prices down, and
    overloads the client with choices.
  - Failure mode: line-item proposals.
- **Price integrity**: present $50,000, not $49,785.
  - Rule: a price for strategic work ends in three zeros. Less strategic
    implementation sold by the hour or deliverable can drop to round hundreds.
  - Why: a price that implies a formula invites the client to use the formula to
    bring it down (rates times hours prompt complaints about the rate or the
    hour count).
  - Response when asked how a round number was reached: it is what the firm
    charges organizations like this one for work like this.
- **Capacity management** (after Ron Baker's airliner analogy): treat about 10
  client slots as finite seats, segmented like an airline from first class down
  to deep-discount fares, with the discount seats sold only from excess
  capacity. The goal is not a full plane but maximum profit over a period, even
  at partial capacity.

## Concepts

- **Value driver**: a distinct form of value a client may buy or forgo, each
  with its own effect on price.
- **Segmenting**: dividing a market into cohesive groups. Airlines segment the
  market; a design firm segments client by client.
- **Bundling**: packaging the drivers most important to a segment into one
  offer.
- **Yield management**: pricing to get the most revenue from finite capacity.
- **The dozen-client limit**: a customized services firm of any size can manage
  roughly a dozen distinct client relationships.
- **Client tiers**: one to three top-tier clients, a drop-off, then second-tier
  clients, then small, troublesome clients below a line that usually falls
  between the eighth and twelfth.
- **Churn management**: growing by replacing smaller departing clients with
  larger new ones rather than adding clients.

## Anti-patterns

- **Itemized pricing**: enables comparison, invites cherry-picking, and crushes
  margin.
- **Prices that look calculated**: they reveal a formula the client will attack.
  A client who says a round number looks made up usually means they can't see a
  formula to argue down.
- **Unplanned client growth**: every client past 10 to 12 dilutes the firm's
  ability to go deep. The worst-behaved clients should leave first.
- **Mismatched seats**: giving discount buyers first-class value, or letting
  first-class clients pay discount prices.

## Example

Ask a team whether they would rather present $50,000 or $49,785. Most choose the
lower, formula-looking number. Present $50,000 instead, and if challenged, say
it is what the firm charges organizations like this one for this kind of work.
The only comeback left is whether a smaller client would pay less, which ch05
answers: yes.

A companion exercise: impose a hard cap of 10 clients and watch what changes.
The firm starts asking early how much a client will spend, sales shifts from
acquisition to managing the portfolio, innovation becomes the way to grow
revenue per client, and the worst client sinks to the bottom of the list.

## Takeaways

1. Write the firm's own value driver inventory; expect to find a dozen things it
   has been giving away.
2. Bundle drivers into whole options and never let a client shop the list.
3. End strategic prices in three zeros, explain them by what the firm charges
   clients like this one, and stop there.
4. Draw a line on the client list, cap active relationships at about 10 to 12,
   and churn toward larger clients.
5. Segment capacity like an airline. Sell discount seats only when seats are
   empty, and never with first-class service.
6. Grow by engagement size, not client count: ten clients at $200k beat twenty
   at $100k.
