## One-page proposal

- **When**: every unpaid proposal.
- **How**: three option columns (four at most), the highest price last on the
  page, and prices at the bottom. Leave out rationale, bios, hours, and terms.
  Present it in person or on a call; decline requests to just send it. In the
  closing conversation, facilitate rather than close: restate the desired future
  state and its value, walk from the anchor down to the low option naming the
  trade-offs, then ask which option the group is leaning toward.
- **Trade-offs**: it depends on a finished value conversation and an agreement
  in principle, and it fails when sent unattended.

## Four-step value conversation

- **When**: after The Flip, before any price is written.
- **How**: (1) commit the client to the desired future state, then confirm the
  recap is right and complete; (2) agree on metrics; (3) agree on the value of
  success, in profit; (4) give pricing guidance as a range from $X to $Y. Never
  mention solutions. Book the closing conversation with every decision maker
  before leaving.
- **Trade-offs**: senior clients join only for creative, transformative work;
  operational work tends to get rates and dates.

## Anchoring against guaranteed value

- **When**: setting the top of the range ($Y).
- **How**: ask whether a stated share of first-year profit, such as a third,
  would be fair if the firm could guarantee the outcome. If the client offers a
  much smaller share, ask whether they would really pass on an investment
  guaranteed to return several times its cost. If the client asks whether the
  firm will actually guarantee it, stay open but noncommittal: the question was
  about what a fair return looks like.
- **Trade-offs**: quantify soft value only slightly past your comfort.

## Money-no-object anchor

- **When**: building the anchor for every proposal.
- **How**: price what the firm would do if the client had no budget limit.
  Introduce it as a thought experiment about an unconstrained budget that might
  spark ideas. Present it first and never leave it out.
- **Trade-offs**: it rarely sells, which is the point. If it sells 20% of the
  time or more, raise every price.

## Call before sending

- **When**: any time a price is about to be written, including for existing
  clients.
- **How**: call ahead and give the number, for example that the estimate is
  nearly done and will come in at $7,500. Invite objections now, while they are
  cheap.
- **Trade-offs**: none. An objection that the client can't afford it, heard low
  in the organization, is a cue to reach the executive, not a verdict.

## Home Depot middle option

- **When**: the client has in-house staff for execution and the firm wants a
  high-margin middle option.
- **How**: the low option is a diagnostic (findings and recommendations); the
  middle option is concepts or strategy plus unbundled help forms (knowledge
  transfer, oversight, standards, reporting, open access); the anchor is full
  implementation. Price the middle against the anchor, not against cost.
- **Trade-offs**: if the anchor won't sell anyway, push it higher to flatter the
  middle option.

## Something-for-everyone ladder

- **When**: you can't tell a price buyer from a poker player.
- **How**: the low option is a stripped time block of junior hours (fixed
  minimum, paid in full up front, no top-ups by the hour); the middle option
  holds the value drivers this client wants; the anchor sits on top. Show what
  the low option gives up instead of discounting.

## Paid diagnostic

- **When**: scoping needs audits, interviews, or research, or the $X to $Y range
  is too wide.
- **How**: after giving pricing guidance, sell one branded diagnostic with no
  options. Promise that the findings will come with priced options for the next
  phase. Loss aversion keeps the client engaged once the work starts.

## Open access

- **When**: the client wants ongoing strategic access to the principals.
- **How**: charge at least $5k a month (Enns's floor), with the client
  initiating contact, and offer 10% or more off for 12 months paid in advance.
  Add it to the middle option or the anchor.
- **Trade-offs**: never divide the annual budget by 12. Before proposing any
  retainer, ask whether you are overvaluing revenue certainty, whether it is
  right for both sides, and whether you chose it only because it is easier to
  sell. A yes to the first or third question, or a no to the second, means
  reconsider.

## Lease the deliverable

- **When**: the client balks at a lump sum and the deliverable behaves like
  software, or the creative IP has ongoing use.
- **How**: ask whether the client would pay a monthly amount rather than the
  lump sum ($3k a month for 36 months instead of $50k is $108k). Protect
  front-loaded cost with a deposit or a financed-amount clause. For creative IP,
  retain ownership and license its use.

## Guarantee and tip clause

- **When**: a guarantee suits a single executive value buyer; a tip clause suits
  any engagement and replaces the guarantee when many stakeholders are involved.
  Plant both at agreement.
- **How**: guarantee client by client, never as firm policy, and carry only risk
  you can sleep on. For phase one, use pay-upfront-with-a-guarantee.

## Speak the risk

- **When**: presenting options along the risk spectrum.
- **How**: name the risk level of each option, start with the lowest-risk option
  for the client, and always price one option above the stated budget.

## Alternatives to cutting price

- **When**: pushback on price.
- **How**: answer affordability with payment terms, risk with a guarantee,
  unwillingness with removed value (for good clients choosing higher options
  only), and volume with a trade for a larger written commitment. Otherwise walk
  away, treat it as a pause, and come back within days with a new structure.

## Courtesy discount

- **When**: a rare discount for a loyal client.
- **How**: the estimate and invoice show the real price, a line labeled courtesy
  discount, and the final amount. Ask the client what amount would help; it is
  often smaller than you would have offered.

## Invoke policy

- **When**: the client makes unreasonable process demands, or pushes on price or
  timing.
- **How**: state the position as policy, for example that the firm writes
  proposals only after meeting the decision makers. Meet a client policy with a
  firm policy and let the people involved resolve the standoff.

## Procurement playbook

- **When**: procurement arrives after the closing conversation.
- **How**: assume the firm has won and start with no. Out-wait the waiter: if
  they take a day, take two. Trial-close before any concession. At the verbal
  yes, forecast procurement's call and ask the client whether you may refer
  procurement back to them. Decide give-gets in advance. Answer outlandish asks
  with a conditional yes or by asking how they would expect you to do that. When
  told they must get three bids, invite them to get the bids, with no hard
  feelings.
- **Trade-offs**: if procurement arrives before the closing conversation, price
  decides the outcome; consider not taking part.

## Untether from time

- **When**: moving a cost-based firm toward value pricing.
- **How**: give new clients new models and old clients their old prices.
  Value-price strategy first, and discount commodified re-application work if
  you must. Track time off rather than time worked. Use timesheets only when the
  quarterly firm metric (profit or AGI/FTE) misses its target. After every
  engagement, ask how hindsight would change the options, models, and prices.
