# Chapter 16: Sprints, Points, Tips, Pay What You Like, and More

Paraphrased summary of chapter 16 of Blair Enns's _Pricing Creativity_, on
alternative pricing models.

## Core idea

Fixed fees, options, and retainers aren't the only ways to charge. This chapter
recommends satisfaction guarantees, tip clauses, and fixed-price discovery
followed by incentive-backed sprints; rejects points; and treats pay what you
like as a useful lesson that is too dangerous to adopt as is.

## Frameworks

- **Pay what you like** (Executive Shine, a shoeshine stand at Denver's
  airport): customers pay what the shine was worth to them.
  - When: a firm has so few clients that a couple who paid nothing could sink
    it, so adopt the adapted versions below rather than the pure model.
  - Why it works: because their income rides on how customers feel about the
    whole visit, the staff pair an excellent shine with good conversation, and
    they seem to make around $80 an hour. Prices also send signals: offering to
    accept less signals confidence in the value delivered.
- **Satisfaction guarantee** (David Maister's unconditional guarantee):
  - How: tell the client at the outset, and repeat on each invoice, that an
    unhappy client may reduce the fee to whatever they believe the work
    deserved.
  - It differs from a performance guarantee: the client, not a metric, decides
    what counts as success. It sits on top of other pricing models.
  - Why: the agreed price stands unless the client invokes the guarantee, and
    the protection is worth a premium.
  - When: executive consulting with a single value buyer, where the risk of
    abuse is low.
  - Failure mode: the client contact is replaced by a cost cutter, or middle
    managers and multiple decision makers get involved.
- **Tip clause** (Ron Baker, _Implementing Value Pricing_): the mirror image of
  a guarantee. It gives the client no safety net but invites them to pay more.
  - How: after the client selects an option and agrees to the price, add a
    clause in which the client agrees to review the engagement at the end and
    decide whether an additional payment is warranted, given their satisfaction
    with timely, professional service.
  - Why (Robert Cialdini's reciprocity): no client offers extra money on their
    own, so the suggestion has to be made when the deal is struck. It leaves a
    slight sense of obligation that comes due when the client feels grateful.
  - When: with several decision makers or middle managers in the mix, prefer the
    tip clause to a satisfaction guarantee, and attach a tip clause to every
    guarantee the firm offers.
- **Points** (a digital-marketing trend in which deliverables carry point values
  drawn from a purchased block): dismiss them. A universal points system already
  exists, called dollars. Points only shield the salesperson from direct
  conversations about money and value, which are mandatory. Drop the device and
  master the value conversation instead.
- **Sprints** (short Agile work cycles, usually a week or two, staffed by a
  dedicated mixed team): just another way of charging for cost.
  - The Agile argument: because product work can't be planned in full, its price
    can't be fixed in advance. Enns agrees Agile builds better products but not
    that it dictates how to charge.
  - Jon Lax, formerly of Teehan+Lax, argued that sprints improve how products
    get built but not how a firm gets paid for performance.
  - Lax's model: a fixed price for the initial discovery phase, then sprints
    backed by milestone incentives, such as launching a minimum viable product
    or reaching an agreed definition of product-market fit. The incentives for
    hitting milestones must outweigh the incentive to stretch the engagement
    out, the open-tab problem of time and materials.

## Concepts

- **Signaling power of prices**: a price carries a great deal of information
  about buyer and seller (Russ Roberts, _It's a Wonderful Loaf_).
- **Satisfaction guarantee**: the client may pay what they think the work was
  worth if not completely satisfied; added on top of an agreed price.
- **Performance guarantee**: payment tied to a defined result; less client
  discretion than a satisfaction guarantee.
- **Tip clause**: a pre-agreed invitation to pay more for exceptional results.
  Together with the satisfaction guarantee it forms one dial, with protection
  for the client at one end and upside for the firm at the other; set both.
- **Reciprocity**: a delayed exchange of favors; it must be invited at the start
  of the exchange or it is lost.
- **Points**: an unnecessary middle currency between value and money. Whoever
  wants them is avoiding the value conversation.
- **Sprint**: a fixed time box. Selling sprints is selling time, which is
  cost-based.
- **Milestone incentives**: the payment triggers in Lax's model, such as a
  product launch or product-market fit.

## Anti-patterns

- **Pure pay what you like for a firm**: a firm has too few clients to survive
  several who pay little or nothing.
- **Hoping clients will tip unprompted**: they won't; plant the idea at
  agreement.
- **A satisfaction guarantee with many stakeholders or a cost cutter**: invites
  abuse.
- **Points systems**: complexity that hides from the money conversation.
- **Assuming Agile requires time-and-materials sprint pricing**: separate the
  delivery method from the pricing model.
- **Sprint incentives weaker than the incentive to drag on**: the open-tab
  problem remains.

## Example

At Executive Shine in Denver's airport, every chair is busy and a line still
forms. Enns wears his most-worn shoes when connecting through Denver and
sometimes has a second pair shined. He pays $15 to $20 where a shine elsewhere
costs $5 or $6. Because pay tracks the whole experience, the staff deliver a
better shine and real conversation, and one shiner put three children through
college. In an ordinary shine, money changes hands and both sides forget each
other. The lesson for firms: capture the signaling and motivation effects
through guarantees and tip clauses rather than the pure model. Ask whether the
firm's team would behave differently if pay depended on the client's whole
experience.

## Takeaways

1. Offer Maister's satisfaction guarantee when one senior value buyer owns the
   engagement.
2. Propose Baker's tip clause as the deal is agreed, and pair it with any
   guarantee.
3. Reject points-based pricing and hold the value conversation in dollars.
4. On Agile projects, set a fixed fee for discovery, then reward the sprint
   phase for reaching milestones such as a launch or product-market fit.
5. Size those rewards so finishing pays better than dragging the work out.
6. Remember that offering to accept less can signal confidence; use it
   deliberately, with the right clients.
