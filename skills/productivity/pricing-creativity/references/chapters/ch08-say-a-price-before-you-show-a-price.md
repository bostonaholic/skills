# Chapter 8: Rule #4, Say a Price Before You Show a Price

Paraphrased summary of chapter 8 of Blair Enns's _Pricing Creativity_.

## Core idea

Give pricing guidance aloud, in conversation, before any price appears in
writing. The client should hear a price before seeing it, so objections surface
early, when they are cheap to handle and the firm still has the nerve to walk
away.

## Frameworks

- **Rule #4, say a price before you show a price**: always give pricing guidance
  verbally before putting it in writing.
  - When: every proposal, estimate, statement of work, and change order, for new
    and long-standing clients alike.
  - How:
    1. Before going off to write anything, state the price or range in
       conversation, ideally as the fourth step of the value conversation
       (ch09).
    2. Surface and overcome every price objection before presenting a proposal.
    3. For routine work with existing clients, call first: say the estimate is
       nearly ready and give the figure, then send the document.
    4. If you have to leave a voicemail or text, explicitly invite a
       conversation; you want to hear the objection.
  - Why: early objections help and late ones hurt. Bad news is cheaper early, as
    in investing and hiring. Sunk cost makes walking away harder the deeper the
    firm is in the sale, so the earlier money comes up, the better the
    decisions.
  - Failure mode: saving the money talk for a long proposal with the price on
    the last page, then watching the client flip straight to that page during
    the presentation.
- **Rule of Money** (from Enns's _Win Without Pitching_): those who avoid
  talking about money don't make it. Talk about money early and often, and learn
  to say large numbers as calmly as small ones.

## Concepts

- **Pricing guidance**: a spoken price or range given before anything is written
  down.
- **Early vs. late objections**: objections raised before writing are cheap and
  actionable; after it, they are expensive and tangled up with sunk cost.
- **Sunk cost bias**: the more the firm has invested in a sale, the harder it is
  to walk away from a poor fit.
- **The etiquette myth**: the taboo on discussing money applies to personal
  finances. In commercial conversations, avoiding money reads as weak business
  sense, and claims that the local culture is different are usually discomfort
  in disguise.
- **Stress comes from avoidance**: money conversations feel stressful mainly
  when they are being put off. Steven Pressfield makes the same point about
  writing: the hard part is starting.
- **The decision-maker ladder**: budget limits and objections heard lower in an
  organization often vanish higher up, where executives can expand budgets for
  sound investments.
- **Salesperson vs. pricer**: the person who must have the money conversation
  may not be the one who sets the price.

## Anti-patterns

- **Emailing the price to avoid the conversation**: removes the chance to hear
  and handle the objection.
- **Saving the price for the proposal presentation**: guarantees late
  objections, sunk-cost pressure, and wasted travel and preparation.
- **Relaxing the rule for existing clients**: it applies equally. Emailing a
  statement of work without a call breaks it, and the skill fades without
  practice.
- **Treating every budget objection as final**: executives routinely override
  lower-level budgets.
- **Blaming culture**: confuses personal-finance etiquette with commercial
  norms.

## Example

An agency principal tells a client the fee would be $70k. The client's marketing
VP objects that the budget has no room for $70k. The CEO corrects the VP: the
company has whatever it takes to do the work properly. The objection was real at
the VP's level and absent one rung up. Saying the price early, in front of the
executive sponsor, let the true budget surface before a proposal was written.

## Takeaways

1. Never let a client see a price they haven't already heard from you.
2. Overcome price objections before writing a proposal, not after presenting
   one.
3. Call before sending any estimate or statement of work, even for routine work
   with old clients.
4. Practice saying big numbers flatly and often; the stress comes from
   avoidance, not from the conversation.
5. Treat a budget objection as information about that person's authority; reach
   the executive before treating it as a ceiling.
