## Core idea

The people who sell creative work are usually untrained negotiators. Take
pricing authority away from the front lines, invoke policy instead of
preference, and give pricing to whoever is least overinvested in the deal, which
is often not the principal.

## Frameworks

- **The car dealer model**: the salesperson never has authority to accept less
  than the sticker price, even for a number the manager has already approved. He
  must take every offer back to the manager, and often returns with a security
  bump, one more small ask that removes any doubt the buyer offered too much.
  - When: inexperienced or overeager staff are agreeing to deals the owner would
    have refused.
  - How: design the process so the person negotiating cannot set or approve a
    price. That person can say, with a self-deprecating smile, that they aren't
    allowed to set prices or talk about money.
  - Why: it eliminates bad deals by design rather than by discipline.
- **Invoking policy**: a policy is simply a decision made in advance. Policies
  beat preferences every time, and clients don't negotiate against a point of
  policy.
  - How: before the meeting, write the decision down in formal language (scope,
    price, timeline). When the client asks for concessions, pause, consult the
    document, restate the price and timeline as policy, and hand it over.
  - Meet policy with policy: when a client says its policy bars proposing firms
    from speaking with the decision makers, reply that the firm's own policy
    requires a conversation with the decision makers before any proposal. A
    standoff between two policies forces the people involved to make a judgment
    call and work it out.
  - Rule: use more policies, more often. In Enns's career, no client ever told
    him to just break his own policy.
  - Failure mode: entering negotiations armed only with preferences and
    inclinations. The client's policy always beats the firm's preference.
- **Who should hold pricing authority**: someone not overinvested in the sale,
  the client, or the work.
  - Exclude: the creative in love with the craft, the account person in love
    with the client, the new-business person desperate for a win, and at many
    firms the principal. At more than half of creative firms with persistent
    margin problems, the principal is the main source of price cuts.
  - How to decide: ask the team, who are the best judges of who prices well. It
    may be more than one person. Ron Baker suggests a chief value officer or an
    internal value council (_Implementing Value Pricing_).
- **Make the next price perfect** (pricing review): after every value-priced
  engagement, and especially during the move away from cost-based pricing, hold
  a standard review. Ask how, with hindsight, you would change the options, the
  pricing models, and the prices if you were pricing this engagement again.

## Concepts

- **Security bump**: the extra small ask after checking with the manager, used
  to confirm the buyer didn't underbid.
- **Policy**: a decision made in advance, presented as outside the negotiator's
  authority. Clients walk around a policy rather than pushing on it.
- **Policy standoff**: two conflicting policies, resolved by human judgment
  rather than by concession.
- **Overinvestment**: an emotional, relational, or effort-based stake that leads
  a person to cut price. The best test for pricing authority is who wants the
  deal least.
- **Chief value officer and value council**: Baker's centralized pricing roles.
- **Pricing review**: a retrospective after each engagement on its options,
  models, and prices.

## Anti-patterns

- **Letting the seller set price in the room**: untrained people get
  outmaneuvered.
- **Backing down whenever the client invokes policy**: answer with a policy of
  your own.
- **The principal as price-setter by default**: often the worst offender.
- **Stating positions as preferences**: what the firm would prefer loses to what
  the firm's policy is.
- **No review after the engagement**: the same mispricing repeats.

## Example

Enns prepared for an identity project meeting with a client known to push on
price and speed. He wanted $20k and eight weeks. An hour before the meeting he
typed a two-paragraph policy on identity design (scope, $20k, eight weeks),
printed it on three-hole-punched paper, and filed it in a binder. In the meeting
the client asked for less money and less time. He asked for a moment, read his
policy, said that the price was $20k and the timeline eight weeks, and handed it
over. She read it, handed it back, and agreed.

## Takeaways

1. Take pricing authority away from the front line; most people relieved of it
   are glad.
2. Write prices, scope, and timelines as policies before the meeting, and invoke
   them.
3. Meet client policy with firm policy, and let the people involved resolve the
   standoff.
4. Ask the team who should set price, and suspect the principal first.
5. Weigh appointing a chief value officer or a value council.
6. After every value-priced job, ask how you would price it again.
