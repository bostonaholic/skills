# Chapter 3: Inputs, Outputs, or Value

## Core idea

A creative firm can sell one of three things: inputs (time and materials),
outputs (deliverables at a fixed price), or value (the client's desired future
state). The same engagement can carry very different prices depending on which
one is sold. The real money, and the way past the efficiency ceiling, is in
selling value.

## Frameworks

- **Three things to sell**:
  1. **Inputs**: hours times a rate, plus pass-through costs. The client carries
     the risk of overruns.
  2. **Outputs**: a fixed price for a deliverable. The firm carries the risk,
     the client buys price certainty, and the price is padded to match.
  3. **Value**: a price tied to the client's expected economic outcome. The firm
     sells the desired future state.
  - When: default to value. Inputs and outputs fit specific cases covered later,
    such as tactical or commodified components.
  - How: identify what the client is actually buying, who carries the risk, and
    what price that supports. Choose deliberately instead of defaulting to
    inputs.
  - Failure mode: pricing outputs as inputs plus a buffer is still cost-plus
    pricing. Only tying price to client value escapes it.
- **Value chain** (after Nagle and Holden): cost-based producers go from product
  to cost (hours times rates plus profit) to price, then hope for value.
  Value-based marketers go from the client's challenge to the value created, to
  the price, to a cost ceiling (for example 60% of the price), and only then to
  the product.
  - When: at the start of every engagement, before anyone estimates hours.
  - How: (1) name the client's challenge; (2) quantify the value in revenue or
    profit; (3) set the price; (4) derive the cost ceiling; (5) design the
    product last.
  - Why: every scoping decision then flows from the client's value rather than
    from the firm's costs.
- **AGI/FTE**: AGI divided by full-time equivalent headcount, the one number
  that shows whether a firm sells inputs and outputs or value.
  - Benchmarks (US, as reported in the book): about $160k is respectable, per
    David C. Baker; the median sits near $150k; the observed range runs from
    roughly $70k to $425k. Efficient firms maximizing billable hours land
    between $160k and $200k. A gap just above $200k suggests a practical ceiling
    for efficiency. Value pricers cluster from $250k to $300k and up.

## Concepts

- **Blended rate**: an average hourly rate across billable staff, used to turn
  estimated hours into a price.
- **Price certainty**: what a fixed price buys the client; worth a premium
  because it removes surprises.
- **Desired future state**: the client's outcome, such as loyal customers and a
  set amount of new annual profit. It is what a value pricer sells.
- **Cost-plus pricing**: covers both input and output pricing. Nagle and Holden
  call it a simple route to profitability in theory and to mediocre results in
  practice.
- **Value-based pricing**: setting the fee by what the work does for the
  client's customers and profit, regardless of the hours or deliverables behind
  it.
- **Culture of waste**: the slack that efficient firms forbid but that
  predicting and creating the future requires.
- **Who holds the risk**: inputs leave it with the client, outputs move it to
  the firm, and value is a shared bet on an outcome.
- **The silent room**: when a team goes quiet after quantifying a client's
  outcome, it has just found value it was about to ignore.

## Anti-patterns

- **Estimating hours first**: it locks the team into cost-plus thinking before
  anyone asks what the outcome is worth.
- **Fixed price as estimate plus buffer**: it feels like progress but sells the
  same inputs with more risk.
- **Understanding value pricing without practicing it**: in one group, all 30
  firm owners said they understood value pricing and one practiced it. The only
  business case against value pricing is that it is harder: harder math,
  conversations, cost accounting, and project management.
- **Value-pricing a middle manager**: value conversations need senior decision
  makers responsible for future value, not budget holders.
- **Efficiency as strategy**: the most efficient firms are the most exposed to
  disruption, because they watch their own production instead of their clients'
  changing needs.

## Example

A UX firm prices a customer-loyalty app for a restaurant chain three ways.

| Method  | Basis                                                                                       | Price                                            | Risk   |
| ------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------ |
| Inputs  | 300 to 400 hours at a $200 blended rate (midpoint 350 hours, $70k) plus $5k of user testing | $75k estimate, billed monthly on actuals         | Client |
| Outputs | Top of the range plus a buffer, fixed                                                       | $90k, a $15k premium for price certainty         | Firm   |
| Value   | The VP of Marketing expects $8M in new sales, about $1M a year in profit                    | $200k, a fifth of the expected first-year profit | Shared |

To present the value price, tie the fee to the outcome: the firm commits to
building an excellent app for the chain's customers, one that also adds about
$1M a year in profit, and its fee is a fifth of the first year's gain.

## Takeaways

1. Compute the firm's AGI/FTE. Below about $200k, the firm is selling inputs or
   outputs whatever it calls them.
2. Default to value pricing; keep inputs and outputs for tactical, commodified
   components.
3. Start each engagement with the client's challenge and its dollar value, then
   set the price, then a cost ceiling of about 60%, then the scope.
4. Reach the senior decision maker responsible for future value; value pricing
   fails when sold to budget holders.
5. Accept that value pricing is harder. Most of the difficulty is practice and
   access, and it pays off.
