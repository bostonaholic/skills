## Core idea

Avoid depending on a single way of charging. A client who refuses a lump-sum
purchase may happily pay a monthly fee to use the same website, app, or even
logo, and over the term that fee can add up to much more than the sale price,
while the client gains the predictable cost it wants.

## Frameworks

- **Lease model**: turn a one-time purchase into recurring payments for use or
  access.
  - When: a price-sensitive client balks at a lump sum; the deliverable behaves
    like software (websites and apps are software) or has ongoing usage value
    (identity, imagery); or the client already pays monthly for everything else
    it uses.
  - How:
    1. Reframe the question: would the client pay a given amount per month,
       rather than a larger amount up front?
    2. Build a multi-option proposal with different value drivers in each
       option, not just different payment terms.
    3. Protect front-loaded costs: charge for at least some development up
       front, or write the contract so development is a financed amount due
       regardless of later cancellation.
    4. For identity and other creative IP, offer a lower-priced initial design,
       retain the IP, and license its use back to the client, as photographers
       do.
  - Why: a monthly payment turns a variable or lumpy cost into a fixed one, and
    clients pay a premium for that price certainty. Ongoing payment also keeps
    the firm motivated to keep improving the product, which is a better
    experience for the client and more profitable for the firm.
  - Failure mode: treating the deal as a pure lease when the costs are
    front-loaded. Without a financed-amount clause, the firm carries all the
    cancellation risk.

## Concepts

- **Lease model**: recurring payment for use rather than a one-time transfer of
  ownership.
- **Financed amount**: contract language that makes up-front development due
  whatever the client later decides. Arguably this is financing rather than
  leasing, a point Enns doesn't contest.
- **Retained IP with a usage license**: the designer keeps the rights to the
  logo or website and the client pays for its use, potentially indefinitely; one
  designer earned enough in royalties to nearly retire.
- **Power by the hour**: the jet-engine service model (pioneered in the 1960s
  and later adopted by Rolls-Royce, GE, and Pratt & Whitney), with a fixed fee
  per flight hour covering servicing and parts. It turns variable maintenance
  cost into a fixed one.
- **Price certainty**: turning a variable cost into a fixed one; a benefit worth
  a premium.
- **Model lock-in**: assuming there is only one, or only a few, ways to charge
  for a deliverable.
- **Treat it like software**: a website or app is software built for one
  customer, so charge like a subscription. If the client pays monthly for all
  its other software, sell the firm's the same way.

## Anti-patterns

- **Selling when the client would rather lease**: most web and app firms still
  do, and lose the price-sensitive buyer entirely.
- **Retainers for everyone, or sales for everyone**: the same failure in
  opposite directions, model lock-in.
- **Forcing a good idea into the wrong model**: Enns has watched promising,
  lucrative work fall apart this way.
- **Leasing front-loaded work without protection**: no up-front charge and no
  financed-amount clause.

## Example

A design firm principal had a price-sensitive client who wouldn't pay $50k for a
new website. Enns asked whether the client would pay $3k a month; she thought
so. She built a multi-option proposal with different value drivers in each
option, and the client signed at $3k a month for 36 months. A client who refused
$50k paid $108k. The caveat: the development cost was front-loaded, so the
contract had to bill some development up front or make it a financed amount due
regardless.

## Takeaways

1. Before concluding that a client won't pay, ask the question again as a
   monthly number.
2. Treat websites and apps as software and offer monthly licensing as one of the
   options.
3. Consider retaining IP and licensing its use for identity and other creative
   assets when the client fits.
4. Always cover front-loaded costs in the contract when leasing.
5. Sell the price certainty (a variable cost made fixed) as a benefit, not a
   concession.
6. Question the default model regularly; leases, licenses, royalties, and
   financing are all available. Changing the pricing model moves price more than
   haggling over the number does.
