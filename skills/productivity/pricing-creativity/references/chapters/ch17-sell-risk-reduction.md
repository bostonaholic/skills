## Core idea

Every option the firm presents splits risk differently between the firm and the
client, and a premium price is an insurance premium. Let the client choose their
own level of risk, and in the closing conversation speak openly about the risk
each price carries instead of quietly pricing it in.

## Frameworks

- **Two meanings of premium**: in sales, an amount paid above a benchmark price;
  in insurance, an amount paid for protection from risk. They are the same
  thing, so the higher-priced options are insurance policies. The biggest risk
  is the financial risk of poor performance: the client wonders whether the firm
  will get it right, and what it will cost them if it doesn't.
  - Why: Peter Drucker argued that business profit comes from taking risk. The
    client is risking resources in the market and hires the firm to remove some
    of that risk, and only the client knows how much they will pay to remove it.
- **Risk spectrum of options**:
  - Most client risk, least firm risk, lowest price: selling capacity or time.
  - Least client risk, most firm risk, highest theoretical price: guaranteed
    value, the number anchored in the value conversation.
  - Everything between is the trade-off zone: the more risk the firm removes,
    the higher the premium.
  - How: place each of the three options on the spectrum, and name where it sits
    when presenting.
- **Let the client choose their risk level**: assume you have misjudged how much
  risk the client will tolerate, and let the options do the sorting. That is yet
  another reason never to send a single price.
  - When: in every proposal. Enns confesses that for over ten years he charged
    consulting clients the same premium regardless of their tolerance for risk.
- **Speak to the risk, don't just price it**: when presenting, tell the client
  there are three ways to work together toward the agreed goals and value. One
  is low risk for the client, at the highest price (say $100k). One meets the
  stated budget (say $40k) but leaves all the risk with the client. One sits
  between (say $65k) and shares the risk more evenly. Explain each, starting
  with the low-risk option, then ask how much risk the client is comfortable
  carrying and how much they want the firm to take away.
- **Always price one option above the stated budget**: late in a sale, clients
  weigh the consequences of failure more heavily than the value of success
  (early in the sale it is the reverse), so as they near a decision they become
  more willing to pay extra to make risk go away.

## Concepts

- **Premium**: price above a benchmark, which is insurance against risk.
- **Risk premium**: the increment the client pays for each unit of risk the firm
  absorbs.
- **Guaranteed value**: the top of the spectrum, with all delivery risk on the
  firm.
- **Capacity or time**: the bottom of the spectrum, with all risk on the client.
- **Trade-off zone**: the range of options between the two ends.
- **Risk profile**: each client's own appetite for risk, unknowable in advance,
  so offer choices. The option a client picks reveals their real appetite.
- **Late-sale loss weighting**: near the close, fear of failure outweighs hope
  of gain.
- **The proposal is spoken**: the one-page document supports the conversation,
  and the conversation decides the option.
- **The rental-car counter**: navigation, collision coverage, prepaid fuel,
  winter tires, an upgrade. The firm, like the counter clerk, asks which risks
  the client wants to keep and which it wants removed. The anchor is the fully
  insured option, and the low option leaves the client on their own.

## Anti-patterns

- **Imposing your own risk profile**: like a mechanic who decides not to replace
  a worrying part to save the customer money, adding worry, the opposite of what
  the customer wanted.
- **One-size-fits-all premiums**: the same insurance in every proposal, whatever
  the client.
- **Pricing risk silently**: folding the premium into the price unnamed, which
  leaves the client looking at nothing but a bigger figure.
- **Capping options at the stated budget**: forgoes the late-sale willingness to
  pay for certainty.
- **Treating the document as the proposal**: the words in the room do the work.

## Example

Three options for a client with a $40k stated budget:

| Option                  | Price | Risk                                |
| ----------------------- | ----- | ----------------------------------- |
| Low risk for the client | $100k | The firm absorbs most delivery risk |
| Balanced                | $65k  | Shared risk                         |
| Matches the budget      | $40k  | The client carries all the risk     |

Walk through them in this order, opening with the safest option for the client
and describing which worries each price removes. Afterward, discuss what the
exercise revealed about the client's tolerance for risk. Pricing the first
option far above the budget is deliberate.

## Takeaways

1. Treat every higher-priced option as an insurance policy, in your own mind and
   in the client's.
2. Map the three options along the spectrum from capacity to guaranteed value.
3. Use the word risk when presenting each option.
4. Put at least one option above the budget the client named.
5. Leave the choice of which risks to keep to the client.
6. Ask which risks the client wants removed, and adjust the options to match.
