# Chapter 5: Rule #1, Price the Client, Not the Job

Paraphrased summary of chapter 5 of Blair Enns's _Pricing Creativity_.

## Core idea

Nothing a firm does has value until a specific client perceives it, so the same
work can rightly command different prices from different clients. Never answer a
question about what the firm charges for a service without first knowing which
client it is for.

## Frameworks

- **Rule #1, price the client, not the job**: never get trapped naming a price
  for a service in the abstract; the price depends on the client.
  - When: every engagement at a customized services firm, and every time someone
    asks for a price early.
  - How: (1) learn the client's context and the value the firm could create
    before naming any number; (2) hold yourself and colleagues to it, since
    offering a price early is the clearest sign of a slide back to cost-based
    pricing; (3) justify price differences by value, never by inputs. Claims
    about extra meetings rarely explain a gap as large as the one between a
    national automaker and a local dealership.
  - Why: perceived value varies enormously from one client to the next.
  - Failure mode: charging everyone the same for the same thing, the worst
    mistake in value pricing.
- **Minimum level of engagement (MLOE)**: the threshold below which the firm
  doesn't take a client.
  - Formula: about 10% of the annual AGI target. A $1M firm sets about $100k; a
    $10M firm, about $1M. It follows from the ten-client capacity limit; adjust
    for growth plans and comfort with turning prospects away.
  - Usage: state the MLOE firmly in sales conversations as a negotiating lever
    (for example, a minimum of $100k in fees over 12 months, or projects that
    typically start at $25k). Publish softer ranges or examples on the website.
- **Publish pricing guidance, not prices**:
  - Wrong reason: proving affordability. Competing on price signals low quality,
    and penetration pricing only works for productized firms with scale
    advantages.
  - Right reasons: signaling exclusivity (pricing is positioning) and qualifying
    prospects in advance, so clients too small to serve don't fill the contact
    form.
  - Right methods: the MLOE, or typical ranges and examples, such as typical
    project sizes and typical annual client spend. Never specific prices for
    specific services.
- **Customized or productized**: decide which the firm is. The muddled middle
  brings only commodification and eroding margins.

## Concepts

- **Price discrimination**: some buyers will pay more and therefore should; the
  same logic sits behind senior discounts and cheaper medicine for poorer
  countries.
- **Willingness to pay** (Hermann Simon): the buyer's ceiling. Simon once
  scolded a shop clerk for discounting a camera he was ready to buy at full
  price; her job was to let him pay it.
- **Early prices are low prices**: the sooner in the sale a price appears, the
  lower it tends to be.
- **Penetration pricing**: cutting prices to gain share; workable only with
  production or scale advantages.
- **Pricing as positioning**: high stated budgets signal quality. Upscale firms
  state the scale of their engagements, not a price for a logo.
- **Customized services firm**: every client is a blank slate. Selling is
  consultative, cycles are long, and the sale itself is the sample of the work.
  It scales through churn and a rising MLOE.
- **Productized services firm**: standardized, finite offerings sold
  transactionally by product-style salespeople; scalable in theory, and easier
  to sell as a business (John Warrillow, _Built to Sell_).
- **No client, no value**: like the tree falling with no one to hear it, the
  work has no value until a client perceives it.

## Anti-patterns

- **Answering a price question with a number**: it cuts off the information
  gathering that value pricing depends on.
- **Explaining higher prices with inputs**: more meetings never account for the
  real gap.
- **Publishing low prices**: broadcasts low quality, low confidence, and low
  sophistication.
- **Tiered packages on a customized firm's website**: copying software-company
  pricing pages without pursuing that kind of scale, common among digital
  agencies that resell software.
- **Being both customized and productized**: the muddled middle is pure margin
  erosion.

## Example

The ch04 exchange continues. Told that the price is what the firm charges
organizations like theirs, the client asks whether a smaller company would pay
less. Under Rule #1 the answer is yes. A design firm shouldn't charge a local
car dealership what it charges a national automaker's brand division, because
the logo's value differs by orders of magnitude. The justification is the
client's size, revenue upside, and risk, not extra hours.

## Takeaways

1. Learn the client's situation before naming any figure, because a number
   offered early tends to be a low one.
2. Explain why one client pays more than another by what the work is worth to
   each (size, upside, risk), not by effort.
3. Set the MLOE at about 10% of the annual AGI target; use it as a lever in
   conversation and publish ranges on the website.
4. Publish pricing guidance only to position the firm upscale or to screen out
   prospects too small to serve, never per-service prices.
5. Choose customized or productized and run the matching model; only productized
   firms publish tiered packages.
6. Grow by raising the MLOE and replacing small clients with larger ones, not by
   adding clients.
7. Remember that discrimination runs both ways: the same job may be very
   profitable with one client and below cost with another good one. Lifetime
   value decides, not the math of a single job.
