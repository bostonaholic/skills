# Chapter 19: Never Discount (But When You Do, Do It Right)

## Core idea

Never discount is the rule. Treat it as a guideline to break only for loyal
clients, never to win business, and when you break it, document the discount so
it can't become the new price.

## Frameworks

- **Discount vs. price cut**: both sides see a discount as rare and driven by
  circumstance; a price cut sets a new price level from then on.
  - When: before conceding anything, name which one is happening.
  - How: if the client will expect this price again (a new client, a first job,
    a price buyer), it is a price cut; accept the lower margin permanently or
    refuse. Call it a discount only when it is a one-time favor to a good
    client, documented as such.
  - Failure mode: calling a cut to win the deal a one-time discount. The lower
    number becomes the benchmark the price only falls from.
- **First-Job Rule**: the first job with any new client must be profitable, even
  though over the relationship it matters more that the relationship is
  profitable than that any one job is.
  - Why: margin, like negotiating power, shrinks over time. Planning to make it
    up later is either self-deception or dishonesty (sneaking in margin the
    client doesn't expect). A price buyer won't turn into a value buyer after
    the first project.
- **Discounts as favors** (Cialdini's reciprocity): reserve discounts for good,
  loyal clients who pay well and understand the firm's need for profit, for
  example an underfunded pet project.
  - How: when the client says thanks, don't wave it off as nothing. Say you're
    glad to help and that you trust they'd return the favor someday. Tying the
    gift to reciprocity at the moment of thanks keeps it on the ledger.
  - Why: it keeps the relationship from sliding into one side asking or
    commanding and the other complying with growing resentment. There is no
    obligation ever to collect.
- **Document every discount**: every estimate and invoice shows (1) the real
  amount, (2) the discount, labeled as a courtesy discount, and (3) the final
  discounted price.
  - Why: the documentation is the firm's defense when the client forgets that
    last time's price was discounted. Point to the invoice, which records the
    reduction as a one-off for special circumstances, and offer to resend it. If
    the accounting system can't show this, change the accounting or never
    discount.
- **Ask the client to name the discount**: never guess the number the client
  wants.
  - How: acknowledge the client's circumstances, say a one-time discount may be
    possible, and ask what amount would help.
  - Why: the request is often smaller than what you would have offered. If it is
    too big, counter lower.

## Concepts

- **Discount**: a one-time concession for special circumstances that both sides
  understand won't recur.
- **Price cut**: a concession that resets the price level for all future work.
- **Courtesy discount**: Enns's label on estimates and invoices for a documented
  one-off discount.
- **Balance of trade**: the informal tally of who has done what for whom; naming
  reciprocity keeps the tally even.
- **Price buyer vs. value buyer**: a client won on price stays a price buyer.
- **Negotiating against yourself**: offering a concession the client hasn't
  asked for (see ch22).
- **Free work as a gift**: doing a small, worthwhile project (a holiday card, a
  charity campaign) at no charge for a very best client, to build loyalty.
- **A discount is a favor to a friend**: granted rarely, to those who deserve
  it, with reciprocity implied. A client who always asks is neither a friend nor
  a good client.

## Anti-patterns

- **Discounting to win a new client**: signals the firm isn't the expert
  practitioner and sets a permanent ceiling on margin.
- **Planning to recover the margin on future jobs**: self-deception or
  dishonesty.
- **Waving off thanks as nothing**: wastes the moment for reciprocity.
- **Guessing the discount amount**: negotiating against yourself.
- **Hidden or undocumented discounts**: guarantee a future argument that last
  time cost less.
- **Getting carried away with free work**: keep it for surprising and delighting
  the very best clients.

## Example

A good client thanks the firm for a courtesy discount on an underfunded pet
project. The wrong reply brushes it off; the right reply says you're glad to
help and trust they'd return the favor. The invoice shows a $30,000 fee, a
courtesy discount of $5,000, and a total of $25,000. A year later the client
says the work cost $25k last time. The firm explains that the reduction was a
one-off for special circumstances, as the invoice records, and resends it. The
price stays $30k.

## Takeaways

1. Start from no discounts, making exceptions only for loyal clients who pay
   well and are in a genuine bind.
2. Don't use a discount to land a new client; make the first job earn a profit.
3. Ask the client to name the discount amount instead of guessing.
4. Label every discount a courtesy discount on the estimate and invoice, with
   the real price first.
5. When thanked, invite reciprocity instead of brushing it off.
6. Occasionally go beyond the ask and do a small, worthwhile project free for a
   best client.
