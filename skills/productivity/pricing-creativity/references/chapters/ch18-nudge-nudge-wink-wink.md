# Chapter 18: Nudge, Nudge; Wink, Wink

## Core idea

Every proposal influences the client's choice, intended or not. Learn the
cognitive biases at work and use them as choice architecture, ethically and
without taking away the client's freedom to choose, while watching for the same
biases in your own decisions.

## Frameworks

Each bias, with Enns's application:

- **Anchoring**: relying too heavily on the first piece of information received.
  - Apply: lead with a high price, and anchor creative work the same way by
    showing the bolder concept first. Anchor or be anchored: if the firm doesn't
    anchor high, the client anchors low.
- **Social influence and the bandwagon effect**: decisions follow what others
  do, and conformity grows as more people join in, up to a tipping point
  (Malcolm Gladwell).
  - Apply: mark an option as the most popular, or tell the client that most
    clients choose the middle option. Earlier in the sale, show what competitors
    are doing that the client isn't.
  - When a prospect asks why they should hire the firm, don't accept the
    invitation to sell. Offer instead to explain why current clients hire the
    firm, and let the prospect judge whether those reasons apply. That replaces
    your self-serving pitch with the prospect's tendency to follow peers, and
    keeps the firm out of vendor status.
- **Framing, loss aversion, and the endowment effect**: the same information
  leads to different conclusions depending on presentation, and losing something
  you own hurts about twice as much as gaining it pleases.
  - Apply: frame the choice as giving something up rather than acquiring
    something. This explains why guarantees and trial periods work, and why
    people overpay at charity auctions. Key tactic: close on a diagnostic rather
    than the whole engagement. Once the two sides are working together,
    separating is harder than never starting, and the client will rationalize
    continuing even after a rough first phase. Find a way to begin, even with a
    small step.
- **Sunk cost bias**: increasing investment in a pursuit after evidence shows
  the original investment was a mistake.
  - Apply to yourself: ask which expensive new-business pursuit the firm is
    continuing only because of what it has already spent, and stop it.
- **Certainty bias and the Dunning-Kruger effect**: people who say they are 99%
  certain are right only about 40% of the time; the unskilled overestimate
  themselves and experts underestimate themselves. Amateurs push and
  professionals undersell, and buyers sense it.
  - Apply: audit your own and your salespeople's claims about how likely a deal
    is to close, and bet against anything called a sure thing. Certainty without
    data behind it is usually misplaced. The same applies to job candidates:
    hard selling or underselling may reflect Dunning-Kruger rather than
    competence.
- **Scarcity bias**: what is rare seems valuable, and what is abundant seems
  not.
  - Apply: limit and say so, for example that the firm takes on only a few new
    clients a year, or that only two clients can hold open access to the firm's
    CEO. Anything genuinely limited or occasional triggers it; find one for the
    next proposal.
- **Charm prices**: prices ending in 9 signal a sale. In one Richard Thaler
  example, a garment priced at $39 outsold the same garment at $34 and at $44.
  - Apply: never end a price in 9 when charging in the thousands, since it
    implies a formula. But use a charm price to nudge toward the middle option:
    $10k, $19k, and $30k instead of $10k, $20k, and $30k.
- **Confirmation bias**: seeking information that supports existing beliefs and
  ignoring what challenges them; the most common and most powerful bias.
  - Apply: don't exploit it; rise above it. Robert Kegan and Lisa Lahey
    (_Immunity to Change_) describe three levels of mental complexity: following
    others' ideas, authoring your own, and seeing your bias toward your own
    ideas, which very few adults reach. Senior clients at the middle level are
    surrounded by people who validate them, the iceberg of ignorance. The firm's
    opportunity, even its obligation, is to say what the client's own people
    won't, which claims the expert practitioner position. Information alone
    rarely changes a mind, and the more someone thinks they know, the more
    susceptible they are.

## Concepts

- **Heuristics**: mental shortcuts that speed decisions and cause predictable
  biases.
- **Nudge, or choice architecture**: shaping decisions without removing the
  freedom to choose (Thaler and Sunstein).
- **Endowment effect**: ownership roughly doubles perceived value.
- **Tipping point**: a small imbalance that cascades into broad conformity.
- **Charm pricing**: prices ending in 9, which signal a sale.
- **Iceberg of ignorance**: the false confidence of senior leaders whose staff
  tell them only what confirms their views.
- **Two systems**: fast, intuitive thinking and slow, analytical thinking;
  biases live in the gap.
- **A diagnostic as a foot in the door**: once the client starts, loss aversion
  keeps the door open.

## Anti-patterns

- **Failing to anchor**: the client anchors low instead.
- **Accepting an invitation to convince**: relegates the firm to vendor status.
- **Prices ending in 9 at the scale of thousands**: broadcasts formula pricing.
- **Chasing a pitch because of what has already been spent**: sunk cost.
- **Trusting overconfident close estimates**: certainty bias in the pipeline,
  including your own.
- **Pandering to the client's existing view**: forfeits the expert position.

## Example

Three options are priced at $10k, $20k, and $30k, with the middle as the target.
Enns reprices the middle at $19k (a charm price) and tells the client that most
clients choose the middle option (social influence). The $30k option includes
open access to the firm's CEO, one of only two such slots (scarcity), and was
shown first (anchoring). Should the client balk at the whole engagement, sell
the diagnostic first and let loss aversion hold the relationship together.

## Takeaways

1. Anchor high, with price and with the bolder creative concept, or be anchored.
2. Replace the pitch for why to hire the firm with why current clients hire it.
3. Frame choices as losses, and start with a diagnostic if the whole engagement
   won't close.
4. Charm-price the middle option (for example $19k), but never use 9-endings
   that imply a formula.
5. Point out real limits, such as how few new clients or access slots the firm
   offers.
6. Turn sunk-cost and certainty bias on yourself: end bad pursuits and discount
   sure things.
7. Don't exploit confirmation bias; challenge the senior client's view to claim
   the expert position.
